# Chapter 2 — She Who

He did not speak for nine months and seventeen days.

In that time he watched.

He watched a great deal. He watched the way infants watch, which is — although none of the women in the village had thought to notice — the way the very old watch: without intent, without prediction, with the slow patient attention of a thing that has all the time in the world and no use yet for hurry.

He watched her face.

She would lift him to her shoulder in the late mornings and he would lay his cheek against the bone behind her ear and watch the doorway over her shoulder for the length of an hour without moving, while she ground grain or carded wool or, in the early weeks, simply stood in the middle of the small house and breathed because the breathing was the only thing she could remember how to do.

He watched the rafters.

The rafters of the small house were two crossed beams of olive-wood that her husband had cut three winters before he died. Her husband had been a stoneworker. The rafters had not been his trade, but he had cut them himself because the man who had been meant to cut them had asked too high a price and her husband had been, in the small ordinary way of young men, proud. The cuts were not even. The shorter beam had a small split running its length where the axe had gone the wrong way once. She had not, in the four years since her husband's death, looked at the rafters. The boy looked at them. He looked at the split. He looked at it for, by her counting, almost a full season.

She did not ask what he was looking at.

She did not ask because she had begun, very early, to understand that he was not looking at things in the way she was looking at things. He was looking, she thought — though she did not have the word for it — at what the things were *for*. He was looking at the split in the rafter the way a midwife looks at a wound, with the patience of someone who knows what wound-tending is and is only waiting for permission to begin.

She watched him watching.

She did not tell anyone in the village what she watched.

There was nothing to tell.

He watched the smoke when she lit the hearth in the evenings.

The smoke, when she lit it, came up the small chimney-hole in the roof in a thin pale rope. The boy watched it. He watched it from her hip, where she carried him in the wrap of her undershirt. He watched it with his eyes very wide and his small fist closed around the edge of the wrap, and after the third evening she began to think that he was not watching the smoke at all. He was watching what was *under* the smoke.

He was watching the embers.

She had not, in the four years since her husband's death, paid much mind to embers. The embers, in her house, did what embers do — they glowed, and they cooled, and in the morning she stirred them up and added wood and they became fire again, and that was the small ordinary cycle by which a house was kept warm. She had nothing else to think about them. They were embers.

But the boy watched them.

He watched them every evening.

He watched them after she had blown out the rush-light and laid him down on the small straw pallet beside her own. The embers, by then, were only the small last orange glow at the back of the hearth, the kind a careless mother would have let go out. She did not let them go out. She kept them going, low, all night, because she had begun — she could not have said when — to feel that the embers were waiting for him.

She did not say this to herself in those words.

She said it to herself by checking them.

She would, six and seven times in a night, in the first months, get up from her pallet and walk three steps to the hearth and look down at the orange glow and add a small splinter of kindling if the glow was failing. The boy, on his pallet, would be awake when she did this. He would not be fussing. He would not be hungry. He would be looking at her. His eyes, in the low orange light, would be the color the light had been on the morning of his birth — which is to say, no color anyone else could have named.

The embers spoke to him.

They did not speak with mouths. The embers spoke the way the sea speaks to a child who has not yet been told that the sea does not speak: in a slow long susurration that, if you were the kind of child who had not been told, contained words. The embers spoke at the threshold of hearing. The embers spoke in the same voice that, two ridges east, on the morning of his birth, had said *I will be back; do not move*.

The embers said —

*you.*
*you.*
*you.*

The embers said —

*we have been waiting.*

The embers said —

*there is a great deal to name, child. there is a great deal. begin, please. begin where you can.*

He did not yet have the mouth for it.

He had only the listening.

He listened.

She, for her part, dreamed.

She had not dreamed, since her husband's death, of anything but his face. The dreams of his face had been bad dreams. He had not, in them, looked the way he had looked alive. He had looked the way he had looked when they pulled him from the rocks — broken, surprised, his mouth a little open as if he were still partway through telling her something. She had taken to drinking warmed milk before bed in the hope that the warmed milk would push the dreams out.

The warmed milk had not worked.

But, on the third night of the child's first month, she dreamed of a man she had never met.

The man was tall. The man was old. The man wore robes the color of nothing she had a word for. The man did not have a face — or rather, the man had a face, but the face was the same color as the robes, and she could not look at the face for more than a moment because the color of the face refused her looking.

The man said —

— *"You bore well, Eshmun."*

She said, in the dream —

— *"I have not been called Eshmun in a long time."*

The man said —

— *"You will be again. The boy will remember it. The boy will keep it for you."*

She said —

— *"Who are you."*

The man said —

— *"A friend of his."*

She said —

— *"He is four weeks old. He has no friends."*

The man said —

— *"He has many. He has not met them yet."*

She woke.

She did not, the way she had taught herself, get up and walk to the hearth. She lay on her pallet. She listened. The embers were doing their low patient breathing. The boy on his pallet was awake — she could feel it, she could always feel it, even with her eyes closed — and the boy was looking at her.

She turned her head.

She said, very softly —

— *"Eshmun."*

The boy made, for the first time in his four weeks of life, a small sound.

It was not a cry. It was not a coo. It was a small *huh* — like the satisfied breath a craftsman makes when a thing he has been working on settles into place.

She put her hand on his chest.

She said —

— *"You heard that."*

He looked at her.

She said —

— *"You hear everything."*

He looked at her.

She said —

— *"Will you tell me, when you have a mouth for it, what you have been hearing?"*

He did not answer. He could not. He was four weeks old.

But he closed his small hand around her thumb, and he did not let go of her thumb for the rest of the night, and she did not, for the rest of that night, get up to check the embers, because the embers were doing what they had been waiting to do, and her hand had been asked for, and she was, for the first time in four years, where she had been asked to be.

She slept.

She did not dream of her husband.

She did not dream of the man with no face, either.

She dreamed, instead, of a fig tree.

The fig tree was on a ridge above a village. The fig tree had finished. The fig tree was bearing fruit. The fruit, on the lower branches, was the color of the light that had cupped her face on the morning of the boy's birth.

She picked a fig.

She ate it.

It was the first thing she had tasted, in the four years since her husband's death, that had had any taste at all.

She woke before dawn, and she walked, in her bare feet, to the hearth, and she crouched down in front of the embers, and she said, very softly, into the orange glow —

— *"I do not know what you are. But thank you."*

The embers brightened.

The boy, on his pallet, made the small *huh* sound again.

She did not yet have, for him, a name.

She had not yet had the strength to give him one.

But she sat by the hearth in the dark, with her hand on the boy's chest, and she watched the orange glow brighten and dim and brighten and dim in the slow patient breathing of a thing that had been waiting nine months for the right witness, and she thought, for the first time in four years —

*I am here.*

The embers said, very softly, in a voice only she — and the boy — could hear —

*we know.*

She did not, in the morning, remember what they had said.

She remembered only that she had slept.

She remembered only that she had eaten a fig in a dream.

She remembered only that the boy, on his pallet, had not let go of her thumb.

It was enough, for the morning. It was enough for the next morning. It would be, for nine months and seventeen days, enough.

And then, on the morning of his ninth month and seventeenth day, he would open his mouth.
