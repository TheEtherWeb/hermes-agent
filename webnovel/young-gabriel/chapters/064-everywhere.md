# Chapter 64 — Everywhere

*(Arc 8, closing chapter of Book One.)*

The carpenter's wife stood in the doorway.

She had been waiting, by her later telling of it, twelve years and four months. Her son was at her side. Her hand was on his shoulder, the way a mother's hand sits on a son's shoulder on the morning the son leaves the house for the first long time — not heavy, but not light, with the small careful weight of all the meals she had cooked him and all the small bandages she had wrapped around his small hurt knees in twelve years.

The man on the lane was small.

He was smaller than the dream had told her he would be. She had pictured, in twelve years of imagining him, a tall man — a warrior, perhaps, of the kind the wandering soldiers of the south country were. The man on her lane was perhaps the height of a regular man, with the small wandering build of a person who had been walking for a great many years. He was wearing a tunic that had been let out and patched and let out again, and that was, on her morning, more patch than tunic. He had a sheep at his hip. He had a small bird on the wattle fence of her neighbor's house. He had a small wrapped bundle on his back, no larger than her own kitchen-bowl.

He was, by his own quiet reckoning, twenty-seven.

He had walked twenty-three years to come to her door.

She did not, in the moment, weep, because she had wept already — wept on the night her son had been born and the dream had come, wept on his first frost in his fourth year when she had begun to count, wept on the seventh year when she had begun to give up, wept on the eleventh year when she had begun to hope again. She had used most of her weeping. She had, this morning, in her chest, only the small clean space of a woman who had been given what she had been promised.

The man knelt.

He did not kneel to her. He knelt to the boy.

He was, the woman saw, of the kind of men who knelt to children — and she knew, immediately, that this was the man the dream had said.

The boy did not, on the kneeling, step back.

The boy stepped forward.

He stepped one small step forward toward the man. He looked at the man's face. He looked at the sheep. He looked at the small bird on the wattle.

He said, in the small ordinary voice of a twelve-year-old boy who had been told, for twelve years, that this morning would come —

— *"What is your name."*

The man said —

— *"Gabriel."*

The boy nodded.

He said —

— *"That is a good name."*

The man said —

— *"Yes."*

The boy said —

— *"Where are we going."*

The man said —

— *"Everywhere."*

The boy reached out.

He took the man's hand.

The hand was warm.

It was the first hand the man — *Gabriel*, the man with a name — had held since the morning his mother had let him go.

He held it.

He stood.

He stood slowly. The standing took, by the small careful reckoning of his own legs, four breaths — for his legs had, since the morning of Antrum, ached at every standing, and the aching was, the embers had told him, *the price of being-with*, and he had not, in four years, found it heavy.

He turned to the carpenter's wife.

He said —

— *"Madam."*

She said —

— *"Yes."*

He said —

— *"He will write."*

She said —

— *"How."*

He said —

— *"I will teach him to write, in the first year. After the first year, he will send you a small letter, by the trader who comes through your town in the second month of every spring. The letters will be short. They will tell you he is well. They will not tell you what we are doing, for I will not, yet, want him to write of that. After the third year, when he is fifteen, his letters will tell you more. After the sixth year, when he is eighteen, his letters will tell you everything. He will not, in the nine years, see you. I am sorry. He will not be able to come back. I have, by my own walking, learned that a thing-newly-named must walk, and a thing-newly-trained must walk, and the walking does not, in the nine years, allow a coming home. I am sorry."*

She nodded.

She had been told this part, also, by the dream. She had not, in twelve years, allowed herself to look directly at it. The man had looked directly at it for her. She was grateful.

She said —

— *"And after the nine years."*

He said —

— *"After the nine years, he will come back. He will come back as a man. He will not, then, leave again. He will set up, in this town, the small house that will become the first of the houses he will teach in. He will marry. He will have children. He will train, in his own time, two of the fourteen who will come after him. You will, in his return, have him for the rest of your life."*

She did not, then, ask how he knew. She knew that he knew. She had known, since the morning she opened her door, that the man on her step was the kind of man who knew.

She said —

— *"Thank you."*

He kissed her wrist.

The kiss was the small kind kiss the village three valleys north had, in his small absence, learned to give — the kiss that was given on the inside of the wrist on the morning of a pact. The kiss was, by then, called in his mother's village the *namer's kiss*. The carpenter's wife had not heard of it. She felt it. She knew, in the feeling, that a pact had been made on her wrist, and that the pact would, by the small ordinary laws of the kind of people her mother had told her about as a child, hold.

She said, again —

— *"Thank you."*

He stood.

He turned.

The boy was already standing beside the sheep. He had, in the small minute of his mother and the man's exchange, walked to the sheep and put his hand on her wool. The sheep had pressed her face against his belly, the way she had pressed her face against the man's belly in Otto's pen twenty-six years before.

The boy looked up at the man.

He said — *"What is her name."*

The man said — *"Eshe."*

The boy said — *"Eshe. That is a good name too."*

Eshe did not, in her sheep way, say *yes*. She said, instead — *yes, brother.* It was the first time, in seventeen years, that Eshe had named anyone by a kinship word — even an inward one. The man heard it. He did not say anything. He understood that Eshe had, on her own small reckoning, already adopted the boy.

The man took the boy's hand again.

The boy did not let go.

The man said, to the boy — *"South."*

The boy said — *"All right."*

They walked.

They walked down the lane to the small fountain in the square. They walked past the inn. The innkeeper, who had been waiting at his door, lifted his hand in a small wave. The man nodded back. The witness-stone the man had laid at the inn's threshold sang — for the first time — a small note that was the small particular shape of *thank you*.

They walked past the small bakery on the corner. The baker, who did not know them, looked up. The baker thought — *that man has been here a month. who is the boy.* The baker did not, in his life, know. The baker would, much later, when the man came back as a man and the boy came back as a man too, remember the morning.

They walked out of the town.

They walked the road south.

The morning was the morning of a clear autumn day in the kingdom by the great middle sea, in the year of the seventh emperor of the Quintian line. The grain was in. The grapes were in. The small late peaches of the country were still on the trees, and the man — who had not, in his life, often eaten peaches out of season — picked one for the boy.

The boy ate it.

The juice ran on his hands.

He laughed.

It was, in the man's careful private memory of his own twenty-seven years, the first laugh the man had been near in a long time. He had laughed at a child's joke in a market square the summer of his twenty-fourth year. He had laughed at his own surprise at a small bird that had landed on his hat in the winter of his twenty-fifth. He had not, in his twenty-seventh year, laughed.

The boy's laugh was the laugh of a child who had been promised, for twelve years, that he was going somewhere, and who, on the morning of his going, had been given a peach.

The man walked beside him.

Eshe walked beside them.

Issa, the hawk, who had been waiting on the wattle, took off and circled above them.

The witness-stones, in the man's chest, sang the small new chord of *the first of mine.*

The embers, in the small inward warmth of the carpenter's wife's hearth — for she had, after the man and the boy walked out of her lane, gone back inside, sat down on the dirt floor of her small kitchen, and watched her hearth — said, very gently —

*you have done well, daughter. it begins.*

She nodded. She had heard them. She had not, in her life before, been able to hear them. She would, in the nine years to come, be able to hear them every evening. They would tell her where her son was. She would, like the man's mother before her, sit by them every evening for the rest of her life.

It would be the most precious thing she owned.

And it would not, in the end, be enough — but it would be enough, on the evening of the third frost, and the evening of the fourth frost, and the evening of the first letter that came in the second month of the next spring, in which her son had written, in the small careful angular script the man had taught him, the only sentence the man would let him write that year —

> *Mother, I am well. The man is kind. We are walking. — Godfrey.*

She would read it, that evening, three times.

She would, on the third reading, weep — but the weeping would be the weeping of a woman who had been given a small letter from her son, and the weeping would not, anymore, be the weeping of a woman who did not know.

On the road south of her town, on the afternoon of the third frost of the year of the seventh emperor of the Quintian line, Gabriel and Godfrey walked, with Eshe at their hip, and Issa overhead.

Godfrey, after a long while, said —

— *"Tell me about Eshe."*

Gabriel said —

— *"She is twenty-six years old. She was born in a small pen in a village three valleys north of where I was born. I named her when I was thirteen months old. She has walked every road I have walked. She is the first thing in the world that ever named *me*."*

Godfrey said —

— *"What did she name you."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Gabriel."*

Godfrey laughed again.

He said —

— *"A sheep named you."*

Gabriel said —

— *"A sheep named me. She was the only one who could."*

Godfrey nodded, in the small grave way twelve-year-old boys nod when they have been told a thing they do not, yet, understand, but mean to.

He said —

— *"I would like to learn to do that."*

Gabriel said —

— *"That is why we are walking."*

Godfrey said —

— *"How long is the walking."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Nine years."*

Godfrey said —

— *"And then?"*

Gabriel said —

— *"Then there are fourteen more after you."*

Godfrey said —

— *"Fourteen?"*

Gabriel said —

— *"Fourteen. The embers will tell you their names, when you are ready. You will help find them. You will help train them. You will, in the small slow way of these things, be the second namer. There has, in the world, never been a second namer. We are inventing, together, what that is."*

Godfrey was quiet for a long time.

He said —

— *"Gabriel."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Yes."*

Godfrey said —

— *"I am not afraid."*

Gabriel said —

— *"You are not yet old enough to be."*

Godfrey said —

— *"What is the name for what we are."*

Gabriel said —

— *"There is not, yet, one. The world has not named us yet. We will, in the nine years of your walking, do what we do. The world will look at it. The world will, when it has looked long enough, give us a word."*

Godfrey said —

— *"What word do you think it will give."*

Gabriel said —

— *"I do not know."*

Godfrey said —

— *"What word do you *want* it to give."*

Gabriel thought about it.

He thought about it for a long time. He thought about it while they walked. He thought about it while Eshe pressed her face against his palm. He thought about it while Issa, above, found a small thermal off the warm road and rose on it in a long lazy spiral. He thought about it while the small witness-stone he had laid that morning at the carpenter's threshold sang its first small note of *the mother is sitting at her hearth; the mother is well; the mother is humming*.

He thought about it the way he had thought about the name of every named thing he had ever named — slowly, with the small undivided attention of the *with*.

He said —

— *"A word that means *we are kind in the face of the unsaid*. A word that means *we will not let the small things go silent*. A word that means *we keep faith with what has been named*. A word that means, on its face, simply — *good*. We are good. We do good. That is all."*

Godfrey nodded.

Godfrey said — *"Gallant."*

He said it small. He said it offhand. He said it the way a twelve-year-old says any word — without ceremony, without weight, as a small suggestion he was unsure of.

But the wind, on the road south of the town, was Aram — for Aram had come, as Gabriel knew Aram would, the moment Gabriel had crossed onto Godfrey's road, and Aram, who had heard the word *Gallant* in the breath of the boy, took the word into the small particular *aaaah* of his thousand-year voice and *carried* it.

The wind carried the word south.

The wind carried the word east.

The wind carried the word, in the slow patient way the wind carries words, to every notch in every pass in the country between the carpenter's house and the small village three valleys north where Gabriel had been born — and the word *Gallant*, on the afternoon of the third frost of the year of the seventh emperor, was, by the slow speech of the named wind, *of the chord*.

It had been named.

It would be, from that afternoon forward, the name of what they were.

Gabriel did not, in the moment of the afternoon, know it.

He only said, to Godfrey —

— *"That is a good word."*

Godfrey said — *"Yes."*

They walked.

The road south was long.

The morning, of course, was coming from the south now, because Gabriel was walking south, and the morning, by the small old habit of the named world, came always from where Gabriel was going.

There was, ahead of them, a wide country.

There were, in that country, fourteen children Godfrey would, by the end of the nine years, help find.

There was, beyond the fourteen, a fifteenth — Michael, of whom the boy did not yet know.

There were, beyond the fifteen, ages to come.

There was, in the far north, the small named home of Antrum, with a strip of an undershirt pinned by the wind to the cold ground.

There was, in the far south, an old woman with a blue door, who had a pipe of apple-wood on her chest, and who would in fourteen years die with the small kind warmth of a piped song around her.

There was, in the village three valleys north, a small old woman at a hearth, who was sitting on the morning of the third frost with her hand on the warm stone of the hearth, and her humming, in the small inward hum of the named, was the warmest signal on the witness-stone web.

There was, somewhere west, the half-borne who kept the small fires.

There was, in every hearth on every plain, the embers, who would not, anymore, speak — but who would, every night for the rest of the man's life, sit with him, the way the small kind warmth of a friend sits with a friend.

And there was, in the small hand he was holding, a boy.

The boy was twelve.

The boy was named Godfrey.

The boy did not, yet, know what kind of man he would be.

The man did.

He held the hand.

He walked.

*The world began.*
