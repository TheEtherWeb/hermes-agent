# Chapter 7 — A Pact Before Leaving

The last frost came on the morning of the boy's four-year-and-eleven-day.

She had been waiting for it.

She had woken every morning of the previous three weeks and gone to the door of the small house and put her hand on the threshold-stone to feel whether the threshold-stone was cold in the way of a frost-morning or warm in the way of a morning that had no frost, and on every one of those mornings the threshold-stone had been the small particular cold of frost, and she had, on every one of those mornings, gone back inside and built up the fire and breathed.

On the morning of the boy's four-year-and-eleventh day, the threshold-stone was warm.

She stood at the door for a long time.

She watched the small light come up over the eastern ridge. She watched it gather, the way light gathers on the morning of any spring day, into the small pale gold of an early morning that had decided not to be cold. She watched a small bird — a finch, of the kind that nested in the eaves of the woman across the lane — land on the corner of the wattle fence and pick at something with its small careful beak.

She said, to the threshold-stone —

— *"You knew."*

The threshold-stone did not answer. The threshold-stone was, in the small ordinary speech of stones, not the kind that answered. It was the kind that, in the embers' counting, was called *door-stone* — a stone whose work was to *hold the line between in and out*, and whose work it was, in particular, to know when a thing was about to cross.

She went back inside.

The boy was awake.

He was sitting on his pallet. He was already dressed — he had pulled on, by himself, the small wool tunic she had finished weaving for him three weeks before, the small leather sandals Iossa had cut and sewn and brought one evening with the air of a man giving a wedding gift, the small wool cap she had knit in the long evenings of the deep winter. He was sitting on the edge of his pallet with his small hands on his knees, the way he sat in front of the embers, and he was looking at her.

Eshe was at his feet.

Eshe was — and this part the widow had not done, because it had not been hers to do — freshly oiled, with her wool combed straight, and the small leather collar Otto had made for her four winters ago around her neck, freshly conditioned in fat.

Otto, the widow understood, had come over in the night.

Otto, the widow understood, had known.

The boy walked to her.

She had known, in the way mothers know, that he would, that morning, walk to her. She had not, until he did, known how she would receive him.

He put one small hand on her wrist.

He said, very gently —

— *"She who."*

She knew the tone.

She had been waiting for the tone for two years.

She knelt.

She held his face. His face was the small particular face of a four-year-old boy — round still in the cheeks, but lengthening in the jaw, with the same eyes the morning light had been before the morning of his birth. She held it the way a midwife holds the face of an infant whose name has just been spoken — with the small careful attention of a thing one is committing to memory.

She said —

— *"Today."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She said —

— *"Where."*

He looked east.

He did not point.

He had not yet, in his four years, found a use for pointing — for the things he wished to indicate were, in his experience, already known to the listener. The east was known to her. The east was, she had said many times in his hearing, the place the morning comes from. He looked east.

She nodded.

She said —

— *"How long."*

He said —

— *"Until."*

She said —

— *"Until what."*

He said, very gently —

— *"Until the next-to-last thing has been named."*

She closed her eyes.

She had been preparing this sentence in her mind for two years. She had not prepared it well enough.

She said —

— *"Will you come back."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She said —

— *"When."*

He said —

— *"When you call me."*

She said —

— *"How will I call you."*

He looked at her.

He said —

— *"She who. You will know."*

She held him for a long time.

She did not weep.

She had decided, on the night the embers had told her *one more season*, that she would not weep on the morning. She had decided that the weeping would be for the long days afterward, and not for the morning, because the morning belonged to him, and her weeping was hers, and she would not put hers on his.

She packed him a small wrap of cheese.

She packed two figs from the tree under which he had been born, which she had walked the previous afternoon to the ridge to pick herself, and which had been waiting on the lower branches as if they had known her hand was coming.

She tore off, from the same undershirt she had used to wrap him on the morning she had walked down the ridge with him — the undershirt was, by then, the most-washed cloth in the village, a thin grey rag that no longer resembled any garment — a small square. She folded the square. She tucked it inside his small wool tunic, against his small chest.

She said —

— *"Take this."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She said —

— *"It smells of the morning you were born."*

He said, very gently —

— *"I know."*

She said —

— *"Eshe will go with you."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She said —

— *"I have not asked Eshe."*

He said —

— *"You have not had to."*

Eshe, at the door, was standing the way Eshe had stood for two years — patient, alert, with her face turned in the direction the boy had been looking. The small leather collar around her neck caught the early light. The bell that had hung from Otto's father's hand for forty years was, the widow noted, not on her. Otto had taken it off and kept it. The collar Otto had given her was, without the bell, lighter.

The widow rose.

She walked to Iossa's panel, which sat on the small shelf above the hearth, and she took it down. The panel had, that morning, seventeen names on it. Sixteen of them had been carved by Iossa. The seventeenth had been carved by the boy himself the previous evening, in the small angular script Iossa had taught him — for the boy could, by then, write — and the seventeenth name was *IOSSA*, because Iossa had said, in his evening of carving the sixteenth name, *put me on it last*, and the boy had said, *not last, but I will put you,* and the boy had carved Iossa's name on the panel with Iossa's own knife, and Iossa had wept the way the village now understood Iossa wept — without sound, with his hand on his mouth.

She held the panel out to her son.

She said —

— *"This is yours."*

He looked at the panel.

He did not, for a long moment, take it.

He said —

— *"It belongs to the village."*

She said —

— *"You carved them. You named them."*

He said —

— *"They named themselves. I only spoke. The book is the village's. The village will keep the names. I will carry the names in me."*

She lowered the panel.

She set it back on the shelf.

She said —

— *"You will not take anything."*

He said —

— *"I have the figs. I have the cheese. I have the cloth. I have Eshe. I do not need anything."*

She said —

— *"You are four."*

He said —

— *"I am four."*

She said —

— *"You are very small."*

He said —

— *"I have been the right size since the morning."*

She looked at him.

She had heard that sentence before. She had heard it in the dream, on the lips of the man with no face. She had not, until that morning, known her son knew it too.

She said —

— *"He told you."*

He said —

— *"He tells me a great many things."*

She said —

— *"Who is he."*

He said, very gently —

— *"A friend of mine. You will meet him, when it is time. He has been waiting to meet you."*

She closed her eyes.

She nodded.

She did not ask, that morning, who the man with no face was. She would not ask, in the rest of her life, for she would not, in the rest of her life, be ready to be answered.

She held her son one more time.

She kissed the top of his head.

She set him down on his small feet.

She walked, with him, to the door. Eshe walked behind them.

At the threshold-stone — which had been warm that morning, and was warm still — the boy stopped.

He turned.

He looked up at her.

He said —

— *"She who."*

She said —

— *"Yes."*

He said —

— *"You will be all right."*

She did not, at that, weep.

She said, very softly —

— *"I will be all right."*

He said —

— *"And you will not be alone."*

She said —

— *"No?"*

He said —

— *"The embers will sit with you."*

She nodded.

He said —

— *"And Iossa will visit."*

She said —

— *"Yes."*

He said —

— *"And the children of the village will, by next summer, be able to read. You will teach them."*

She had not, until that morning, known she was going to teach the children of the village to read. She knew it the moment he said it. She would, in the next summer, teach the children of the village to read. She would do this for the rest of her life. She would not have known, without him, that she was going to.

She said —

— *"All right."*

He said —

— *"And one more thing."*

She said —

— *"What."*

He took her hand. He turned it palm-up. He put his small thumb on the inside of her wrist, where the vein ran close to the skin.

He said —

— *"A pact."*

She said —

— *"What pact."*

He said —

— *"I will come back. I will come back twice. And the third time, I will come for you. You will not have to walk. I will come. I promise."*

She said —

— *"Boy."*

He said —

— *"It is a pact. The embers will hold it. The threshold-stone will hold it. The fig tree will hold it. I will come."*

She said, very quietly —

— *"All right."*

He bent his small head.

He kissed the inside of her wrist, where his thumb had been.

It was the small kiss the village would, much later, call the *namer's kiss* — the kiss given on the inside of the wrist, where the pulse ran, on the morning of a pact. It would be, for the next several centuries, the kiss the Gallants gave their mothers when they left to ride.

She did not, that morning, know any of that.

She knew, only, that her son had kissed her wrist, and that his small mouth had been warm.

He turned. He stepped onto the threshold-stone. He stepped off of it onto the dirt of the lane. He did not look back.

Eshe walked behind him.

She watched.

She watched him until he was no larger than her hand, and then she watched the place he was no-larger-than-her-hand in until she could not, anymore, see the place, and then she watched the place she could no longer see for a great while longer, because the watching was the only thing she had to do.

When she went back inside, the embers at the back of the hearth were brighter than they had been in four years.

She knelt in front of them.

She said —

— *"He has gone."*

The embers said —

*we know, daughter.*

She said —

— *"He kissed my wrist."*

The embers said —

*we felt it. it is a good pact.*

She said —

— *"He said he would come back."*

The embers said —

*he will.*

She said, very softly —

— *"Will I weep now."*

The embers said, very gently —

*you may, daughter. you have earned it.*

She wept.

She wept for the rest of the day.

She did not weep the way most women wept. She wept the way a woman weeps when she has been entrusted with something she knows she cannot, in the end, keep — and she wept also the way a woman weeps when she has, in the small new grammar of her son, been *kept*. She had not, until that morning, known the two weepings could be the same weeping.

She wept until the small late-afternoon light came in the small high window and the embers brightened to receive her into the evening.

Then she rose. She washed her face. She built up the fire.

She sat in front of it.

She said, to the embers —

— *"Tell me where he is."*

The embers said —

*he is at the ford of the lower river. he is sitting. he is eating one of the figs.*

She nodded.

She said —

— *"And tomorrow?"*

The embers said —

*he will be further.*

She said —

— *"Every day?"*

The embers said —

*every day, daughter. every day. and every night, when you sit by us, we will tell you where he is.*

She put her hand on the warm stone of the hearth.

She said —

— *"All right."*

She would, for the rest of her life, sit by the hearth every night.

The embers would, for the rest of her life, tell her where her son was.

She would, for the rest of her life, know.

It would be the most precious thing she owned.

And it would not, in the end, be enough — but on the evening of the four-year-and-eleventh day, alone in her small house for the first time in four years, with the boy's pallet empty and Eshe's straw bed empty beside it, it was enough for that evening.

It was enough for the first evening.

It was, that night, all she had.
