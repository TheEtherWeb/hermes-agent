# Chapter 11 — Aram of the High Pass

He climbed the pass in his eighth winter.

He had been promised the pass.

The embers had said, in his second year — when the small lower-country wind had asked him for a name, and he had given it *Aren* — that the wind of the *high* pass was an older wind, and that the boy would meet the high-pass wind on a high pass, and that the high pass was far. *Wait,* the embers had said. He had waited. He had walked east for three more years, and then south for one, and then east again, and the high pass had come up on his road in the small slow way the embers had always told him important things came up — without announcement, as a fact.

The pass climbed for two days.

The road was a goat track. There were small low stone cairns at the corners — old, lichen-eaten, the kind of cairns no one in the present age remembered building. The boy stopped at each cairn and laid his hand on the top stone. He listened. The cairns spoke, in the small slow speech of stones — *we mark. we mark. we have marked here for a long time. keep going.*

On the morning of the third day, the boy and Eshe reached the saddle.

The saddle was a small flat place between two peaks. The peaks were not, by the small ordinary reckoning of peaks, very high — perhaps four bowshots above the saddle. The saddle was perhaps the width of two wagons set end to end. On either side, the ground fell away in long brown slopes of scree and small bent juniper, and the eye, in either direction, could see for a great many days of walking.

The wind hit him in the face.

It was a wind the boy had not felt before.

It was old.

It was, the boy understood at once, very old. It was the kind of old a wind can only get by blowing through the same notch for so long that it has forgotten there was ever any other notch in the world.

The wind said —

*you are the boy.*

The boy did not, in the way the embers had taught him, answer with words.

He sat down on a flat stone at the saddle.

He set his hands on his knees.

He listened.

The wind blew. The wind blew the small particular pattern it had been blowing for, by the boy's growing sense of these things, a thousand years. It blew up the southern slope at a steady pace until it reached the saddle, where it accelerated through the notch, and then it blew down the northern slope and dispersed across the plain on the other side. The pattern was older than any village the boy had been in. The pattern was older than the language his mother spoke. The pattern was older than the cairns.

The boy said, in his inward voice —

*how long have you been here.*

The wind said —

*before the cairns. before the goats. before the people made the goats useful. before the trees were small. i have been the wind that passes the notch. i have been here. that is all i have ever been.*

The boy said —

*you have not been named.*

The wind said —

*no.*

The boy said —

*do you want to be.*

The wind was quiet for a long time. It was the kind of quiet only a wind can do — it kept blowing, but the blowing was a kind of waiting. The boy waited with it.

The wind said —

*i have wanted to be named for nine hundred years. i have not, in that time, met anyone who could name me. the small herders who passed have not. the small traders who passed have not. one woman, three hundred years ago, came up the south slope and sat where you are sitting and listened. she sat for one afternoon. she stood up. she walked away. she could hear me. she could not name me. she went down and lived the rest of her life. she did not, in her life, forget the afternoon she sat at my notch. but she did not give me the name.*

The boy said —

*tell me what you would like to be called.*

The wind said —

*i do not know. i have not had, in nine hundred years, any reason to know. i have been the wind. that is all.*

The boy listened to the wind a long while longer.

He listened to its rising on the south slope. He listened to its acceleration through the notch. He listened to its dispersal on the north plain. He listened to the small particular *aaaah* sound it made at the moment of the notch, where the air became fastest and thinnest and sang against the small stones of the saddle.

He said, very gently —

— *"Aram."*

The wind stopped.

It stopped the way a thing stops when it has been hit by a name. The saddle, for the length of one breath, was still. The grass at the edge of the scree did not bend. The small flag of cloud above the southern peak did not move. The pass, in that one breath, was the kind of stillness no high pass had been in for nine hundred years.

Then the wind began again.

It began as the same wind it had been a moment before — the rising on the south slope, the acceleration through the notch, the dispersal on the north plain. But the *aaaah* sound at the moment of the notch was, very faintly, different. It contained, now, the small particular shape of a word.

The wind, in the boy's inward ear, said —

*Aram. Aram. Aram. yes.*

The boy stood up.

He bowed.

He bowed in the small careful way the Nephil had bowed at the ford — not the bow of a small person to a large person, but the bow one named being makes to another.

Aram, the wind, blew across his face the kind of warm gentle gust that was, in the slow speech of named things, a kiss.

Eshe, beside him, lifted her face into the wind.

She closed her eyes.

The boy understood, watching her, that she had been waiting two days for the wind to be named. She had not, in the two days of climbing, told him. She had only walked behind him. She had known the wind was old. She had known the wind was lonely. She had let him hear it, in his own time.

He put his hand in her wool.

He said —

— *"Eshe."*

She said —

*yes.*

He said —

— *"Will you carry a thing for me."*

She said —

*what.*

He said —

— *"I will, when I am older, need to lay a cloth at a place, and the cloth will need to be witnessed. I need a wind that will hold the cloth still after it has been laid. The wind here is the wind that will hold it. I have named him. He has agreed. But the place I will lay the cloth is far from here, in years and in miles. I need to remember, on the morning, who he is."*

Eshe said —

*i will remember.*

He said —

— *"Eshe. You will be very old by then."*

She said —

*i will be old enough. i will remember.*

He nodded.

He said, to Aram —

*will you come, when I call you, in many years, to the far north.*

Aram said —

*you have named me. i will come anywhere you call. the wind goes where the named places need it. i will come.*

The boy bowed once more.

He took his small hip-bag of salt. He took, from it, a small pinch. He flung the pinch into the wind.

The wind took it.

The pinch hung, for a moment, in the notch — caught, the boy understood, in the way the named river had taken his salt at Onerai's crossing — and then was dispersed in the long fast *aaaah* of the wind's voice down the north slope.

The boy turned.

He walked, with Eshe, north down the slope toward the plain.

He walked into the wind.

The wind, on his face, was the small kind warm gust of a thing that knew him.

That night he slept on the north plain.

He sat in front of a small fire he built with juniper branches he had broken on the way down. The fire was small. The embers, in the fire, were not the embers of the widow's hearth — they were *other* embers, of a different small fire — but they were, in the larger sense, the same embers, because every ember in every hearth was the same ember, and the boy was old enough now, at eight, to feel this directly and not only to be told it.

The embers said —

*good, child. you have named a wind.*

The boy said —

*how many winds will I name.*

The embers said —

*four. you have named two. the third you will meet in the southern country. the fourth you will meet at the place we cannot, yet, tell you the name of.*

The boy said —

*the north.*

The embers said —

*the north.*

The boy was quiet for a long time.

He said —

*how old will I be.*

The embers said —

*old. you will be old. you will be twenty-seven. eshe will be sixteen.*

The boy said —

*eshe will be sixteen.*

The embers said —

*yes.*

The boy looked, in the small firelight, at Eshe.

She was lying on the bare ground beside him, with her face on her front legs, asleep in the small steady way she always slept. She was five years old. She would, by the small ordinary reckoning of sheep, live perhaps three or four more years.

He said, to the embers —

*tell me she will be here in sixteen years.*

The embers said, very gently —

*she will. she is not, anymore, an ordinary sheep. she has been with you since the morning of the second year. she has been named by you. she has been witnessed by the river, by the bandit, by the trader, by the nephil, by aram of the high pass. she is, by the count of nameable things, more than a sheep. she will be with you until she is sixteen. she will be with you until the morning after antrum. she will lay down beside the woman you are bringing yourself to, in much later years, and not get up. but she will be with you until then. we promise.*

The boy did not, often, ask the embers for a promise.

He had asked, in his six years of walking, three times.

He had asked once, in the early days, *will my mother be all right*, and the embers had said *yes, child, she will*. He had asked once, after the ford with the Nephil, *will I see him again*, and the embers had said *no, child, he is going home to die,* and the boy had understood, in that moment, what *dying* was, although he had not yet, in that moment, felt grief for it. And he had asked, now, *will she be here in sixteen years*, and the embers had said *yes*.

The third promise was the one the boy would, for the next sixteen years, hold like a small stone in his chest.

He put his hand on Eshe's wool.

She did not wake.

He lay down beside her.

He slept.

The high pass, behind him, kept whispering its long old *aaaah* through the notch — but the *aaaah* now had a word in it.

*Aram.*

The wind of the high pass had a name. The wind of the high pass would, from that night forward, carry the name of every traveler who crossed the saddle into the small ear of every traveler who came after. The wind would do this, in the small honest way of named winds, for a very long time. It would carry, in fifteen years, the name of a small girl — *Gisla* — across the saddle for the first time, although it would not, then, know what the name meant. It would carry, in twenty-three years, the name *Antrum* across the saddle to a small fire on the north plain where a tired man was sitting, and the man would not know it was carrying the name to him, but the wind would.

The boy slept.

Aram, above him, kept its watch.

Eshe, beside him, kept hers.

The embers, in the small fire he had built, kept theirs.

The boy, between them, was as safe as any boy, on any high north plain, in any of the small slow centuries before the world had quite been told most of itself, had ever been.

He slept the deep clean sleep of a child who had, that day, given a thing the word it had been waiting nine hundred years to hear.
