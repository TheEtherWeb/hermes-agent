# Chapter 14 — A Widow's House

He spent the worst winter of his walking in the house of a widow who was not his mother.

He was ten.

The country had been getting colder for two months. The road, by the third week of the first frost, was no longer a road — it was a track under hand-deep snow, with the small frozen ruts of a few cart-wheels showing where, in the autumn, traffic had been. He had been sleeping out of doors. He had been sleeping out of doors well enough — the embers in his small night-fires kept him warm, and Eshe's wool, pressed against his back, kept the cold he could not name out of his bones — but on the morning he came over the small ridge above the village called Vaena, the embers said —

*go in. winter here. find the widow with the blue door. she has been waiting.*

The boy did not, in the year previous to the bandits, have asked the embers questions about their commands. After the bandits — after he had, that afternoon, given two grown men their roads — he had begun to. He said —

*why has she been waiting.*

The embers said —

*she has a pipe.*

The boy said —

*a pipe.*

The embers said —

*you will see.*

He walked into the village.

The village was small — fourteen houses, low and stone, with smoke coming from twelve of them. The blue door was the fifth house on the east side of the small central path. The blue was a small particular blue the boy had not seen on any other door in any other village — a kind of dusk-blue, almost grey, like a piece of slate that had been left out in the rain. He walked to it. He knocked.

A woman opened it.

She was, by his eye, perhaps forty-five. She had black hair beginning to grey. She had the small narrow face of a woman who had been thin all her life and not from hunger. She was wearing a wool dress the color of the door.

She looked at him.

She looked at Eshe.

She said —

— *"Come in. The fire is on."*

He went in.

He did not, that first afternoon, speak much.

She did not ask much.

She gave him a bowl of soup — the kind of soup the small north villages made in winter, with barley and a small handful of the dried beans they kept for the cold and a single piece of mutton-bone that they passed from soup to soup all season. He ate it. She gave Eshe a small handful of barley in a clay dish. Eshe ate it.

The widow's name, the boy learned by the small slow evening, was Maren.

Maren's husband had been a piper.

He had died in the autumn — five months before the boy came — of a fever that had taken three other men in the village. Maren had not had children. She had been in the small house alone for the five months. She had, the boy understood without her saying, been waiting to die. She had not been wanting to die — she was not the kind of woman who wanted things — but she had been waiting, with the small patience of a woman who had nothing now to wait *for*, for the small ordinary winter sickness that would, in the village's reckoning, take an unfed widow soon enough.

The pipe was on the wall.

It was a small pipe of polished apple-wood, with a small bone reed at the top and a string of small bronze rings that had — the boy saw at once — been added one at a time over a great many years, each ring marking a feast at which the piper had played. There were thirty-eight rings.

The boy looked at the pipe for a long time.

He did not, that first evening, ask about it.

He slept on the small straw pallet she set out for him by the hearth. Eshe slept against his back. He woke once, in the night, and saw Maren sitting at the hearth in her dress with her hands in her lap, not weeping, only sitting.

He did not say anything.

He let her sit.

She slept, eventually, in her own bed in the back of the small house.

The boy stayed.

He stayed for the whole of the winter — four months. He did not, in those four months, say much to her at first. He did the small ordinary work of a guest in a winter house — he fed the fire, he carried water from the small well at the back of the lane, he brushed the snow off the small step of the blue door in the mornings. Maren did the small ordinary work of the keeper of the house — she cooked the barley, she counted the mutton-bone weeks, she sat in the evenings with her hands in her lap.

In the second month, she began to speak.

She spoke about her husband.

Her husband had been called Vetor, and Vetor had been the piper of the village for thirty-eight years, and Vetor had had a particular small song that he played, on the eve of the longest night, that the village had loved. Vetor had loved Maren. Maren had loved Vetor. They had not had children, but they had had thirty-eight winters together, and the thirty-eight winters had been, by Maren's quiet counting, the only thing she had ever wanted to count.

The boy listened.

He listened for a great many evenings.

Maren said, on the night of the longest night —

— *"He should have played, tonight."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

Maren said —

— *"The pipe will not play, anymore. I have not played it. He took the playing with him."*

The boy looked at the pipe on the wall.

He listened, the way he had learned to listen, with the long patient ear the embers had given him.

The pipe said, in the small slow speech of made things —

*i was his. i am no one's now. i have not, since he died, been able to sing. i have tried. the small notes are in me still. but the breath that knew how to wake them is gone. i am a piece of wood. i was, once, a piece of wood that knew.*

The boy said, inwardly —

*can you be a piece of wood that knows again.*

The pipe said —

*if a player comes who is willing.*

The boy said —

*does that have to be a player.*

The pipe was quiet for a while.

The pipe said —

*you are asking whether i can be made to sing for the woman, even without a player.*

The boy said —

*yes.*

The pipe said —

*name me. i have, in thirty-eight years, never been named. vetor called me *my pipe*. he loved me. but he did not name me. i was useful. i was the small wooden thing in his hand. i did not — i am ashamed to say — mind. i was so loved. but i did not know what i was, in myself. i know now. i know because vetor is not here, and what i have to be, now, is *the pipe that played for vetor for thirty-eight years*. that is my name. name it. and after, when she takes me down off the wall and breathes on me even with the breath of a woman who does not know how to play, i will, by myself, find vetor's notes in my belly, and i will let them out for her. i will play *for her*. it will be small. it will be plain. but it will be his.*

The boy said —

*i will name you.*

The pipe said —

*then do it kindly.*

The boy stood up.

He walked, in the small firelight, to the pipe on the wall.

He did not take it down.

He put his small hand on the apple-wood, at the place the polishing was thinnest from a man's grip.

He said —

— *"Vetor's-pipe."*

Maren, behind him, made a small sound.

The pipe — *Vetor's-pipe*, now — sang.

It sang very softly. It sang the small particular song the village had loved on the longest night — the boy did not know this, but he heard it begin, and Maren made the small sound again, and he understood. The song was thin. The song was the song of a pipe playing itself, which is not the same as a pipe being played, but the song was *the song* — slow, and careful, and very kind.

It played for a long time.

Maren sat on the dirt floor.

She did not stand up to take the pipe down. She did not move at all. She sat with her hands open in her lap, palms up, the way a woman sits when she has been waiting five months for someone to put something kind into her hands.

The pipe played.

When the song was done, the pipe was quiet.

Maren said, very softly —

— *"Vetor."*

The boy said —

— *"He is not coming back."*

Maren said —

— *"I know."*

The boy said —

— *"But the pipe is here. The pipe is *Vetor's-pipe*, and it will, when you ask it, play for you. I have named it. You will be the one who keeps it. It will not be silent again."*

She wept.

She wept the way the village's quiet widows wept — without sound, with her hands open, with her face wet only at the small inside corners of her eyes.

She wept for the rest of the longest night.

The boy did not interrupt.

In the morning, she took down the pipe.

She held it in her lap.

She put her lips to the bone reed.

She did not, in her forty-five years, have any breath that knew how to play a pipe. She blew, anyway. The pipe — *Vetor's-pipe* — took her clumsy breath, and turned it inside its small apple-wood belly, and let out, in its own time, the first three notes of the longest-night song.

Maren laughed.

She laughed the small surprised laugh of a widow who had not, in five months, laughed.

She played the rest of the season.

She did not become a piper. She remained a widow, with a pipe that had been her husband's. But the pipe played, with her clumsy breath, the small songs that Vetor had played for thirty-eight winters, and the village — by the small ordinary rumor of villages — heard, and came to the blue door in the small dusk-walking of evenings to listen, and Maren did not, that winter, die.

She did not die for fourteen more years.

She died of a small ordinary old age in the small ordinary way of the loved, with the pipe on her chest and the boy — by then a man, by then the Gabriel — at her bedside, having come back to her on the fourteenth winter because the embers had told him *she is asking, child*.

But that, again, was many years away.

On the morning of the spring of the boy's tenth year, he and Eshe stood at the blue door.

Maren was holding the pipe.

She said —

— *"You are going."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She said —

— *"South."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She said —

— *"To your mother."*

He looked at her.

He had not told her about his mother. He had not told her about the village. He had not, in four months, told her where he was from, or that he was a namer, or that he had been walking for six years.

She had known anyway.

She had been a piper's wife for thirty-eight years. She had been, in those years, the kind of woman a man told things to. She knew, in her bones, what a boy who was carrying a long way home looked like.

He said —

— *"Yes. To my mother. For a season."*

She said —

— *"Tell her, from one widow to another, that she has done well."*

He said —

— *"I will."*

He stepped onto the threshold.

She put her hand, very gently, on the top of his head.

She said —

— *"Boy."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She said —

— *"Thank you."*

He said —

— *"Thank you, Maren."*

He stepped off the blue threshold. Eshe followed.

He walked south.

The morning, for the first time in six years, was not coming from the east.

It was coming, the boy understood, from the place he was walking to.

He was ten years old. He had been walking for six. He had not, in those six years, slept in a house for more than a single night until that winter. He had — though he did not yet have the word — homed. He had let himself, for four months, *home*. He had not, until he stepped off the blue threshold, known that he had done it.

He thought, as he walked —

*the road is one direction.*

*the home is the other.*

*they are not, between them, far.*

He kept walking south.

Eshe walked behind him.

The embers, in his chest, said, very gently —

*good, child. you are going home.*

The boy said —

*for a season.*

The embers said —

*for a season. then back to the road. but you have, today, learned the home-direction. you will not forget it. you will need it, much later. on the third return.*

The boy did not, in the spring of his tenth year, know what the third return was.

He kept walking south.

The morning came from the place he was going.

He went.
