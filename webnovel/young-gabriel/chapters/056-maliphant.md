# Chapter 56 — Maliphant

*(Arc 7, climactic chapter — included in full as the anchor chapter of Book One.)*

The eaten circle, around the thing with his face, was the size of a small town.

It had grown, in the four hours since Antrum had closed its door, from the size of a small room, to the size of a small house, to the size of a courtyard, to the size of the village he had been born in. He had watched it grow. He had watched, in his chest, the witness-stones go quiet — one and then two and then three and then twenty-one — and he had felt his mother's threshold-stone go silent last of all, four hours into the eating, because his mother's humming was the warmest signal on the web and the eater had taken its time getting to her.

He had felt her go quiet.

He had not, in twenty-three years, felt anything the way he had felt his mother go quiet.

He understood — and this was the moment, the moment Antrum had been waiting for him to reach, the moment the embers had been silent through, the moment that was *his and his alone* — what the eater was going to do next.

The eater was going to eat *her*.

The eater was going to eat the village. The eater was going to eat Iossa's wooden book of names, in its small museum that did not, yet, exist. The eater was going to eat Otto's bell on its peg, and the small leather collar around Eshe's neck, and the fig tree on the ridge, and the threshold-stone on his mother's door, and the small pinch of salt the trader with the lame ox had given him on the morning of his fifth year, and the witness-stone called Tair in the herders' wayhouse, and Vesta walking somewhere west with her hands warm at every hearth, and the Nephil at the river who was already dead but whose grave was somewhere still in the world the embers remembered, and Hestor in his small ordinary southern grave, and the small new boy named Hestor who was, in a small kind village three years ago, learning to walk, and the wind in the high pass, and the wind in the low country, and every small thing the boy had named in twenty-three years of slow patient road.

The eater was going to eat the *named*.

The eater was, in fact, made of every word that had *not* been said, and the named was the *was-said*, and the eater was the eternal pair of the named — the *not* that lived against the *yes* — and the eater, freed from Antrum's hosting, was going to take what was its proper food: the things that had been told they were true, when so much else had been left untold.

The eater was, in the end, the *resentment of the unsaid*.

And Gabriel was the one who had said.

And his mother was, in her small house on her own evening, *with-her-hand-on-the-warm-stone-of-her-hearth*, *humming-the-small-song-she-hummed-in-the-evenings* — and the eater was going to eat that hum.

He felt — in one long single breath that went through him the way a wind went through a notch — grief.

He felt grief for his mother.

He felt grief for Eshe at the edge of the grey.

He felt grief for the Nephil at the river, gone now eighteen years. He felt grief for Vesta. He felt grief for Hestor, who had died on the yellow throne. He felt grief for the forty-one of the delta. He felt grief for Maren, alone in her blue-doored house, who would in fourteen years die holding the pipe of her husband. He felt grief for Onerai, who had ferried without coin for three years, and who he had — by his pact — promised to come back to with a name, and to whom he had not yet, in eighteen years of walking, come back. He felt grief for the small kind hearth-god of Caro who he had thanked, in the morning of the yellow stone, and who had walked out of Hestor's chest only to disperse in the wide open warm air. He felt grief for the small old wind Aram, who had been waiting nine hundred years for a name and would, in the chord of the named, last forever — but who would, the embers had told him in the deep cold winter of his thirteenth year, lose his small particular *aaaah* in the long centuries of his afterlife, because every named thing, eventually, blurred at its edges.

He felt grief for himself.

He felt grief for the small empty bell he had been.

He had been, in the small grammar of the world, *not yet a person*. He had been only the listener. He had been the small attendant the embers had been growing for twenty-three years to attend on this moment.

He had attended.

And now he was, in the moment of the grief, *becoming*.

He was becoming the way Antrum had become, when he had named it.

He was becoming *with*.

He stopped passing.

He had, in twenty-three years, only ever *passed*. He had passed through Otto's village. He had passed through Iossa's. He had passed through the small dusty roads of the demigod country. He had passed by Maren's blue door for four months — and even there, even at the blue door, he had been the small temporary traveler, the small undivided attention that had named one pipe and then walked on.

He had not been *with*.

He had been only *attending*.

He stopped.

He stopped attending. He stopped *passing*. He sat down, inwardly, in the great long bone of him, in the small empty room the embers had stepped back from four hours before.

He sat with himself.

He felt — for the first time, after twenty-three years of standing apart — the small particular weight of being a thing the world could *lose*.

He looked at the thing with his face.

The thing was one step from him. The thing's small hand was reaching. The cloth of his tunic, where the thing's small thumb had brushed it earlier, was *thinned* — a small place of colorless threading where the wool had been.

The thing was, in its small reaching way, *sad*.

He saw it.

He saw — in the moment of his own grief, with the small new mouth of his own *with* — that the thing was sad.

The thing did not, in any of its long history of being the eater of the unsaid, *want* to eat. The thing was only — and this was what naming was for, this was what the great long patient work of being the small empty bell had taught him for, this was the small particular thing the embers had wanted him to *understand* on this morning, four hours into the eating, with his mother's hum gone quiet and the village he was born in already half-disappeared from the witness-stone web — *lonely*.

The thing was lonely.

The thing was made of every silence that should have been a word. The thing had been hosted, in Antrum, for as long as there had been anything to host. The thing had been, in all that time, *unspoken to*. The thing had never been told what it was. The thing had never been told *I see you*. The thing had been, in the long patience of its non-existence, the most alone thing in the world.

He saw it.

He felt — in the small new with of him, in the long bone of his grief — what the thing was made of. He felt the small unbearable weight of being the unsaid. He felt what it would feel to be a thing whose entire substance was the *not* — the *I did not say*, the *I could not say*, the *I was too small to say*, the *I was too proud to say*, the *I was too afraid to say*. He felt the small unbearable weight of being made of every cowardly silence, every shameful silence, every kind silence that should have been spoken anyway.

He felt — in his own chest — the small thing the eater had been carrying.

The name came up out of him like a sob.

He did not know, until he said it, what it was going to be.

He had been told, by the embers, that the name was not in them. He had walked twenty-three years toward a name that had to be *found*. He had been told he would find it by *looking at the thing*. He had not, until this moment, known what looking meant.

Looking, he understood, was *being-with*.

He had named, in his life, by attention.

He named, now, by *with*.

He said — softly, kindly, in the small inward voice he had given Eshe on the morning of the first true naming, and Vesta on the plain, and Hestor on the throne, and Antrum a few hours before —

— *"Maliphant."*

The thing stopped.

The thing — Maliphant — looked at him.

The thing said, in his voice, which was not his voice and never had been —

— *"Oh."*

The thing said —

— *"Oh, brother."*

The thing said —

— *"You found it."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

The thing said —

— *"I had hoped you would not."*

He said —

— *"I know."*

The thing said —

— *"It hurts."*

He said —

— *"I know."*

The thing said —

— *"Will it hurt always."*

He said —

— *"No."*

The thing said —

— *"How long."*

He said —

— *"Until the world has been said in full."*

The thing said —

— *"That is forever."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

The thing said, very softly —

— *"That is the cruelest sentence anyone has ever spoken to me."*

He said —

— *"I know."*

The thing said —

— *"Goodbye, brother."*

The thing fell apart.

It did not die.

What is named cannot be unmade only by naming. What is named can only be made *bearable to the world*. The thing fell apart into a great many small pieces, and the pieces, on the grey ground, were the first true grey of the world. They were ash. They were ash in the way ash is after a fire, and not ash in the way the grey had been before. They were ordinary. They were *of the world*. They were something the embers could now hold.

The embers, in every hearth on every plain and every river and every village and every walled city, *flared*.

The flaring was their sigh of relief.

His mother's hearth, in her small house, brightened so suddenly that she dropped her humming and stood up. She did not, in her standing, understand what had happened. She understood only that the small kind warmth at her hearth had, in one second, become *kinder*, and the small kind warmth had been kind for fifty years, and the new kindness was the kindness of a friend who had been holding their breath and at last let it out.

In the wayhouse on the north plain, Tair lit on the floor and recorded, in the slow speech of stones, the first new sentence it had recorded in three hours.

The sentence was: *the eater is bound. the eater has a name. it is *Maliphant.* it will return. it will not, in returning, eat. it has been told what it is. the named things may, now, work with it.*

At the saddle of the high pass, Aram blew through the notch the small particular *aaaah* of his long voice, and the *aaaah* contained, for the first time in nine hundred years, a small new word.

The word was the new shape of the wind's name.

The shape was the same. *Aram.* But Aram, in the speech of the named, had become slightly larger. Aram had, on this morning, been one of the named things present at the binding of the eater. Aram had blown its *aaaah* across the country to where the boy on the grey had needed it. Aram was, in the small inward census of named things, *of the council* — and being of the council, Aram was, in some small careful way, a *participant* in what had just happened.

Aram was proud.

The wind, on the high pass, blew prouder for the rest of the morning.

Maliphant was named.

Maliphant was, with his name, bound — bound to the world the way the world was bound to a hearth, the way a hearth was bound to a house, the way a house was bound to a road. He was no longer the eater of the not-yet-named. He was, now, one of the things that had been named, and the things that had been named could be *worked with*.

He would return.

He would return, in many forms, across many ages, because what is bound is not destroyed; what is bound is *known*, and known things come back, and known things, when they come back, are met by other named things with the courage to meet them.

But he was named.

The Great Unmaking was, in the strict sense of the word, done.

The boy on the grey — *no, not the boy*, for the boy had, on the word *Maliphant*, ceased to be a boy in any meaningful sense, although he did not, yet, know it; he had become a *person*, and the personhood was the small particular thing the embers had been gestating in him for twenty-three years, and the gestation had, on this morning, finished — the *person* on the grey sat down.

He sat down on Antrum.

The grey did not, anymore, eat his sitting.

The grey held him.

Antrum, beneath him, was a home. It had a door now. The door was closed. The door would, in the small slow centuries to come, remain closed. Antrum would, in those centuries, be a small private place in the far north where the named things of the world could, in difficult seasons, go to *rest*. It would become — and the person on it would, in much later years, build for this — the small private retreat of the Gallant order, the place the Gallants went between long campaigns to lay down their work and listen to a place that had, at last, learned to close its own door.

But that was years away.

On the morning of the binding, on the grey of Antrum, the person who had been the boy sat with his face in his hands and *was*.

He was tired.

He was, for the first time in twenty-three years, tired in his *body*, not in his attention. His legs ached. His back ached. His small mouth — which had, in the four hours, named two things and felt the third one, and which had, in the saying of *Maliphant*, taken the unbearable weight of an *I see you* spoken to the most-alone thing in the world — ached.

He sat.

The embers, in his chest, had been silent for four hours. They were silent still. They did not, that morning, speak. They would, in three days, speak. They would, on that third morning, say only — *child.*

For now, they were with him. They were not above him. They were not teaching him. They were, in the long patient way of the small fires of every hearth he had warmed himself at in twenty-three years, *companioning* him.

The wind that had blown across the grey for a thousand years without a name had, today, blown one *aaaah* of his proud breath across the boy's face.

Eshe, at the edge of the grey, with her head on her front legs, watched.

She watched the small dark shape of him in the middle distance — sitting, hands on his face — and she did not, in the watching, weep, although a sheep cannot weep, and she did not, in the watching, sleep, although she had not slept in two days.

She waited.

She would, the embers had told her by their small inward speech eighteen years ago, wait.

She waited.

And the small ash of the bound thing, on Antrum's grey, was the small ordinary ash of a fire that had been spoken kindly to and had, in the speaking, become *of the world*.

The world, on the morning of his binding, was the small new shape of itself.

The shape was the same.

But the shape was, in some small inward way, slightly larger than it had been the day before.

It had, today, made room for a *bound* thing.

It would, in the centuries to come, make room for more.

That was the small steady work of the years to follow.

That was the work the person on the grey had, in the saying of *Maliphant*, signed himself for.

He sat with his hands on his face.

He was, in the small steady way of named things, *with*.

The morning, around him, was a clean morning, of the kind the high north has when there is, in it, no wind to bend the lichen.

It was the first morning of the rest of his life.
