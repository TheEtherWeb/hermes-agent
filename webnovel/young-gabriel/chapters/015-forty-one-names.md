# Chapter 15 — Forty-One Names

He did not go home, after Maren's house, in his tenth spring.

He had walked, in his first six years, only east. The walk south from Maren's, in the small early days of the thaw, had felt like a coming-home walk; he had thought, on the morning of the blue threshold, that he was going to his mother. He had been wrong.

He had walked south for three days. He had come, on the morning of the third day, to a small dry crossroads where the road south branched into a southwest and a southeast. He had sat down at the crossroads. He had built a small fire of last-year's stubble. He had waited for the embers to tell him which way.

The embers had said —

*not yet, child. not yet. she will see you at thirteen. not before. you have, still, things to learn before you go to her. you have to learn the kinds.*

The boy had said, in his inward voice —

*the kinds.*

The embers said —

*the kinds of beings. you have named one nephil. you have not yet named a demigod. you have not yet named a small spirit of place. you have not yet named one of the seventeen kinds of stone. you have named one stone, but one kind only. you have, before you come to your mother, work.*

The boy said —

*how long.*

The embers said —

*three years.*

He had, at the crossroads, taken the southwest road.

The southwest road was, the embers said, the road of the demigod country.

He walked it.

He walked it for two and a half years.

He did not name a demigod in those two and a half years. He did not, in fact, meet one. He walked through a great deal of the southwest country — through the small olive-villages of the upper country, through the small wine-villages of the middle, through the small dry villages of the lower, and at last out onto the long flat winter plains of the deep south. He met, in the walking, a great many people. He named a few small things.

He named a spring — the small hot spring in the valley called *Olta*, which had been bubbling out of the ground for a great many centuries without a name, because the villages around it had thought of it only as *the warm spring* and had not, in any of those centuries, sat with it long enough. He sat with it for an afternoon. The spring told him its name was *Carah*. He said it back. The valley around the spring grew, in the next ten years, a kind of red flower that had not, before, grown there.

He named a hawk.

The hawk had come to him on the small dusty plain east of the wine-villages. The hawk had landed on a stump beside the road he was walking on, and had looked at him with the small precise yellow eyes of a hawk who had decided to wait for someone, and the boy had stopped, and Eshe had stopped beside him, and the hawk had said — in the small clipped speech of birds of prey — *i am not from this country. i am from the high places. my mother was a hawk of the heights. my father was a hawk of the heights. they died last summer. i have come down to learn the speech of the low country. you will name me. i will fly above you, when you call, for the rest of my life.*

The boy had said —

— *"Issa."*

The hawk — *Issa* — had taken off. She had circled him three times — high, fast, the small precise circles of a bird who had been named — and then flown south.

She would, in the years of his walking, return on the boy's call from any distance. She would, in his fifteenth year, scout the country before him as he approached the yellow-stone city. She would, in his nineteenth, find the village by the great middle sea where Godfrey would, in three more years, be born, and would scout it for him and come back with the count of houses and the location of the carpenter's door. She would, in his twenty-third, be the only living thing that crossed onto the grey with him to the edge of the not-snow, and she would, when Eshe sat down at the boundary, fly up — fly, in fact, straight up, in a long high spiral — and circle above him while he walked into the grey, and Aram the wind would hold her aloft, and she would be the only one, of all his named animals, who would ever see Antrum from above.

But that was thirteen years away.

In his tenth, eleventh, and twelfth years, the boy walked the demigod country and named small things.

He named twenty-three more witness-stones.

He laid them at thresholds, fords, wayhouses, and crossroads. The web in his chest, by the time of his twelfth winter, was a small singing thing. He could hear, in any quiet moment, the murmur of two dozen places at once. He could hear his mother's threshold — Aede, the stone he had laid there in his eleventh year on his second-attempt journey home, which had been, again, turned back by the embers — saying, every evening, *she has, today, taught two more children to read*. He could hear Tair in the wayhouse on the north plain — *another night, another fire, the elder of this valley has died.* He could hear the stone he had laid at the high-pass saddle, where Aram blew — *Aram blew. another traveler crossed. she was a small woman with a child on her hip. the child had a red shawl. she did not speak. she crossed.*

He listened to the witnesses, in the evenings.

He named, in the second of his three years, the small spirits.

The small spirits of place — the spirits of the bend, the spirits of the ford, the spirits of the threshold, the spirits of the fig. He had named, by his twelfth year, eleven of them.

He could not, by his eleventh autumn, name a demigod.

The demigods, the embers said, did not, like the stones and the winds and the small spirits, *come to* the namer. The demigods *hid*. They had been hiding, most of them, since long before the boy was born. They had been called *bastard* in the villages of the lower country. They had been called *witch* in the villages of the upper. They had been called *spirit* and *wraith* and *unclean* and many other things in many other places, and they had, in the small bitter way of a kind that had been mis-called too many times, gone quiet. They lived in the small overlooked places. They lived in the country between countries. They had stopped, in three or four generations, expecting to be approached at all.

The embers said —

*you must find them. they will not find you.*

The boy said —

*how.*

The embers said —

*we will tell you the names. we know the names. we have been hearing them whispered at the small frightened hearths of the small frightened villages for a thousand years. we have been waiting to teach them to you. we will teach you, now, all forty-one. you will, in your life, name many of them. you will not name all. the ones you name will know the others. the ones you name will go to the others and say *the boy is alive. the boy is the one*. by the time you are very old, the kind will, mostly, be named.*

The boy, on the night the embers said this, was sitting at a fire on the dry edge of the southern plain.

He was eleven.

He sat very still.

He said —

*tell me.*

The embers told him.

They told him for a great many evenings.

They told him the names in the small slow order the embers used for important things — one name per night, with a small explanation in the morning. The first name was *Vesta*. The embers said, very gently —

*the woman who keeps the small fires. you will meet her first. she is the warmest of them. begin with her. she will tell the others.*

The boy said —

*where.*

The embers said —

*on a plain. in winter. with a peach in her hand. you will see the fire from a long way away — it is the fire that has no wood, that has been burning in the open for two hundred years, waiting. she does not, yet, know she is waiting. she thinks she is, only, lonely. you will tell her. she will weep. you will name her. she will go away to every hearth in the world. she is the warmest of the forty-one. you are very lucky to start with her.*

The boy said —

*the others.*

The embers told him the other forty.

The boy wrote them down.

He wrote them, in his small careful angular Iossa-script, on the inside of a small wooden tablet he had been carrying since the wine-villages — a tablet a small old vintner had given him, in payment for the boy having spent an afternoon naming the small spirit of his oldest barrel. The tablet was the size of his hand. The boy used both sides. He wrote, in the small careful list, all forty-one names.

The first name was *Vesta*.

The last name was a name the boy did not, at eleven, know how to pronounce — a name with three of the long *aaaah* sounds in it, the way Aram had had. The embers said, *that one is in the far far east. you will not name him in this life. he is older than you. he will name himself, in time. he has only been waiting to hear that he is one of the forty-one, and we have, by telling you, made it so. when the small message of your tablet reaches him in the slow inward way our messages reach our own, he will know. he will sit down. he will name himself. you will, very faintly, hear him do it, from wherever in the world you are on that day. that is enough for him. that is enough for you.*

The boy nodded.

He closed the tablet.

He put it inside his tunic, beside the small piece of his mother's undershirt.

He said —

*now what.*

The embers said —

*now you walk south, child. you walk south for one more season. you walk to the dry edge of the southern plain. you walk there in winter, because it is on the winter plain that you will see her fire. you go now. you will be eleven when you see it. you have, today, learned the work of the next year.*

He walked south.

Eshe walked behind him.

He was, by then, almost as tall as a man's hip. He was thinner than the village's boys his age — the small wandering thinness of a boy who lived on the road — but he was strong in the wrists, and his small wool tunic, which the widow had woven for him in his fourth winter, had been let out and patched and let out again, and was, by his eleventh, more patch than tunic, but it kept him warm. Issa the hawk flew above him by day. Eshe walked at his hip. The witness-stones sang in his chest. The embers sang at the back of his attention. The four winds — Aren and Aram named, the third and fourth not yet — moved across the country he walked in.

He was as alone, by the world's reckoning, as any boy of eleven had ever been.

He was as unalone, by his own reckoning, as he had ever been since the morning of his birth.

He walked.

He walked south.

The morning came, now, from the south — the morning came, by his eleventh year, from wherever he was going, because the embers had taught him that the morning was a direction that *agreed* with you, when you walked toward what was waiting.

He walked toward a fire that had been burning for two hundred years.

He did not, yet, see it.

He kept walking.

By the second month of his eleventh winter, the plain began.

By the third, the cold deepened.

By the fourth, on a small flat round of grey grass under a low white sky, he saw, in the distance — a small bright point of orange that was not the sun, and not a village, and not any small ordinary fire any small ordinary traveler would have built.

It was tall.

It was the height of a tall man.

It did not move in the wind.

He stopped.

Eshe stopped beside him.

He said, in his inward voice, to the embers —

*there.*

The embers said —

*there, child. go.*

He went.
