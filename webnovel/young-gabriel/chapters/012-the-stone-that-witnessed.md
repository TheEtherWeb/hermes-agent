# Chapter 12 — The Stone That Witnessed

The witness-stone had been in his tunic for five years.

He had carried it, against his small chest, since the morning he had left the village. It was a small flat grey stone, perhaps the size of his palm, with a small whitish vein running through it. He had taken it out of his tunic, in those five years, only at evenings — to set it on the ground beside the small fire he built for the night, and to ask the embers whether it had decided yet what its name was.

The embers had said *no* on every evening for five years.

The embers had said, *the stone is the kind that records what happens near it. it is a witness. it has not, yet, been given a thing to witness. when it has, it will speak its name.*

The boy had thought, in his second year — when he had given Aren the wind a name on the plateau, and Aren had bent the grass — that the stone would name itself then. The stone had not. The boy had thought, in his sixth year — when the Nephil had wept at the river — that the stone would name itself then. The stone had not. The boy had thought, in his eighth — at the saddle, when Aram had stilled — that, surely, the stone would speak.

The stone had not.

It was, in his ninth year, on the north plain, that the stone spoke.

He had not been doing anything special.

He had been sitting at a fire in a small herder's wayhouse — a windowless hut with a stone hearth and a doorless threshold and the smell of forty years of mutton-fat — that the herders of the plain kept stocked with dry wood and a small jar of barley for any traveler who needed it. He had walked into the wayhouse at evening. He had built the fire. He had set Eshe at the hearth-stone. He had taken out the witness-stone, the way he did every evening, and laid it on the floor beside the fire.

He had eaten a small portion of the barley.

He had been about to lie down.

The witness-stone, on the floor, said —

*hold me.*

He picked it up.

He had not, in five years, been spoken to by the stone in any voice he could hear in waking. He had only been told *no, not yet* by the embers. He stopped. He held the stone in his open palm.

The stone said —

*tomorrow, two boys will come into this wayhouse. they will not be travelers. they will be from a village three days east. they will come because the elder of their village told them to come. they will come to wait for a third man, who is wanted in their village for taking a thing that was not his. the third man will come the day after. the third man will sleep in this wayhouse without knowing the two boys are above him in the loft. the two boys, in the loft, will look down and see the man and not know whether to do what the elder told them to do, or to walk back to the village and say they did not find him. they will look down at the man for a long time. they will not, in the end, do the thing the elder told them. they will leave at dawn. the man will leave at dawn. no one will know any of this happened.*

The boy listened with his mouth a little open.

He said —

*why are you telling me.*

The stone said —

*because i am the kind of stone that records. i need to be set down where things happen. if i am set down in a wayhouse, things happen. if i am set down on a road, things happen. if i am set down on a riverbank, things happen. i have been in your tunic. i have not been on the ground. i have not, in five years, recorded a single thing. i am ready.*

The boy said —

*you want to be set down.*

The stone said —

*yes. but where i am set down i will stay. you cannot pick me up again. you will leave me behind.*

The boy said —

*i will lose you.*

The stone said —

*you will know where i am. for the rest of your life. every recording-stone you place from this evening forward will speak to me. we are a single mind, in the small slow way of stones. when you set the second, the second will speak to me, and i will speak back, and you will hear us. when you set the third, the third will hear the second and the first. by the time you have set seven, you will have a — a circle. you will know what is happening at every place you have laid one of us. you will know it from inside your chest. you will not, again, lose any of us.*

The boy said —

*how many will i set.*

The stone said —

*we do not know. as many as you find. they are not common. they are placed on the long roads of the world — one in seven thousand stones, perhaps. we will speak to you when you find us. we will speak in the voice you have learned to listen for.*

The boy said —

*and you. what is your name.*

The stone said, very simply —

*Tair.*

The boy said it back, aloud —

— *"Tair."*

The stone — *Tair* — warmed in his hand.

The boy set Tair down in the corner of the wayhouse, on a small flat place at the base of the wall, where the dust of forty years of herders had collected. He pressed Tair into the dust. He looked at Tair for a long while.

He said, very gently —

— *"Tair. Stay here. Listen. I will know what you hear."*

Tair did not, in the small slow speech of stones, answer in words. Tair settled. The dust at the edge of the stone shifted, by the small breadth of a finger, to make room. The stone was, now, of this place.

The boy slept.

In the morning, he and Eshe walked east.

He did not look back at the wayhouse.

Three days east of the wayhouse, on the small dusty track that ran between two villages, he stepped on another stone of the same kind.

He felt Tair speak.

He felt Tair's voice in the small inward place that had, for nine years, been only the embers' to speak in. Tair said —

*it is one of us. pick him up.*

The boy bent down. The stone at his feet was smaller than Tair — perhaps a thumb-and-a-half across — and it was a slightly different grey. It had no white vein. It had, instead, a small chip in one corner that looked, in the right light, like the small careful chip a careful person might have made on purpose, to mark it.

He held it.

He listened.

The new stone — and Tair, behind him, listening — said —

*Aede. set me on the threshold of a village. any village. i record thresholds. i remember who comes and who goes. when you set me, the village's threshold will be recorded.*

The boy walked into the next village he came to.

He did not announce himself. He walked, with Eshe behind him, to the small flat threshold-stone at the headman's door, and he laid Aede beside it, in the small careful space where a person's foot would, in passing, not touch.

He said, very gently —

— *"Aede. Stay."*

Aede settled.

The boy walked back to the road.

He could feel both stones, now.

He could feel Tair in the wayhouse, three days behind him, recording the small slow life of a herders' refuge. He could feel Aede on the threshold of the village he had just left, recording the small ordinary comings and goings. The two of them spoke, between them, in a very faint inward hum — like two small bells in two small different rooms, ringing in time.

He understood, now, what the witness-stones would, in years, be.

They would be a *web*.

He would, in the years of his walking, place them. He would place them on roads, in wayhouses, at thresholds, at fords. He would place them at the small particular spots where the world's small particular happenings were likely to gather. By his twentieth year, he would have placed thirty-one. By his twenty-third, he would have placed sixty-four. The web would be, by then, the small inward map he carried in his chest of the country he had walked.

It would not be a map of *roads*.

It would be a map of *recordings*.

He would know, by the time he came to Antrum in the grey, what had been said in every wayhouse and on every threshold and at every ford he had ever crossed. He would know, by then, what his mother had been saying at her own hearth, for he had — though he did not yet know he was going to — placed a stone, in his fifteenth year, on her threshold, when he came home for one supper and one night.

He would know things he had not asked to know.

He would know things he could not, in any small ordinary way, unhear.

But on the morning of his ninth year, on a small dusty track between two villages of the north plain, all the boy knew was that he had two witness-stones, in two places, and that they hummed in his chest, very faintly, like two friends humming the same song in two different rooms — and that the world had become, by their humming, slightly larger than it had been the day before, in the way the world becomes slightly larger every time a thing is named.

He walked on.

Eshe walked behind him.

The embers, in his chest, said —

*good, child. begin again.*

He began again.

He began again. He kept beginning. The road east kept being a road. The morning kept coming from the place the morning came from.

And in his small wool tunic, where the witness-stone had lived for five years, the small empty pocket against his chest stayed empty for a long time — but he did not, anymore, miss it.

The world was full of stones.

He would find more.
