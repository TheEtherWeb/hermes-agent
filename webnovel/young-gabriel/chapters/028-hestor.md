# Chapter 28 — Hestor

*(Arc 4, exemplar chapter — included in full as a writing model for the back half of the book.)*

The king's hall was the size of a large barn.

It had been built, in the old years, to hold three hundred people. It had, on the morning Gabriel walked into it, only one. The throne, at the far end of the hall, was of yellow stone. The throne had been carved out of a single block, in the way the old kings of the yellow-stone country had been carved their thrones — from the wall of the original quarry, by the same masons in the same season as the city's gate. The throne was old. The city was older. The man on the throne was, by the small reckoning of the hall, the youngest of the three.

He was fifty.

He had been king for thirty-one years.

He had eaten, by the small private accounting of his own soul, a small kind hearth-god of a small kind village called Caro for the last twenty.

The hall had no guards. The hall had no servants. The king was alone the way kings, in the late years of bad reigns, are always alone. Gabriel had passed, on the way through the antechamber, two empty cloak-stands and a tapestry that had been moth-eaten so long that the moths were now eating the bottom of the moth-eaten parts. There was, at the foot of the throne, a small spilled cup. The wine in the spilled cup had been spilled the previous evening. The wine had been red. The wine was, now, the small brown of dried wine on yellow stone, and no one had come, in the morning, to clean it.

Gabriel walked the length of the hall.

His sandals made the small soft sound a small sandal makes on stone — the only sound in the hall. The king on the throne did not look up.

Gabriel stopped at the foot of the dais. He looked at the king.

He looked at him for the length of three breaths.

He saw — and the seeing was the small careful undivided attention the embers had taught him at the back of his mother's hearth in his ninth month — what he had been told he would see.

He saw a man.

He saw, inside the man, a god.

The god was small. The god was, by the small reckoning of gods, the size of a small candle-flame. It had once been larger. It had been, when the king had first taken it into himself, the size of a small hearth-fire — the warm steady glow of a god of a small village's small evening hearths. The god had been, over twenty years, eaten down. The king had eaten it. The king had not, in any deliberate sense, *eaten* it the way Memnon had eaten Sela — the king had only, in the small private wrong-believing that had begun in the eleventh year of his reign, *been* it. The being-it had, in the slow way, consumed it.

The god, Gabriel saw, was conscious.

The god, Gabriel saw, had been screaming for ten years in a frequency no one in the hall — no one, in any hall — could hear.

The embers had been able to hear it. The embers had carried the screaming, all the way north, to the small ear of the boy on the road. The boy had walked south. The boy had come. The boy was, on this morning, standing at the foot of the dais of the yellow-stone king of a kingdom that was, in three months, going to fall.

The king looked up.

The king said —

— *"Boy."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Hestor."*

The king did not move.

The king's name — his true name, the one his mother had given him on the night of his birth, the one he had not heard spoken aloud since his coronation, the one he had not allowed himself to *think* in a private moment since the eleventh year of his reign — was Hestor.

The king said, in a voice that was, suddenly, not the voice of a king —

— *"How."*

Gabriel said —

— *"The embers."*

The king said —

— *"Boy."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Hestor, the god inside you is dying."*

The king said —

— *"I know."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Hestor, the god inside you was never you."*

The king said —

— *"I know."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Hestor, the god inside you was Vesta's brother. He was the hearth-god of the village called Caro. He came to you the night you were born, in the form of a small fire on the windowsill, because your mother had prayed at his shrine in the morning of her labor — she had walked to Caro in her last week, although the walk was beyond what a woman in her last week should walk, because the shrine of the hearth-god was the small private place she had always gone to when she was afraid — and you have been carrying him, in the small private place of you that you have not been brave enough to look at, for fifty years."*

The king closed his eyes.

The king said —

— *"I know."*

Gabriel said —

— *"You did not, in the eleventh year of your reign, ask to become him. You did not — and this is the part I have come to tell you, Hestor, because no one in the world has ever told you, and you have, in twenty years, been believing the wrong thing about yourself — you did not take him. You did not eat him. You only, in a small bad private moment in the middle of a small private night, said his name in the first person. You said, *I am that god.* You said it because the room had been empty and the night had been long and you had been tired and you had thought, for one tired moment, that no one was listening."*

The king made, in the back of his throat, a small sound.

It was not a sob. It was the small involuntary sound of a man who had been told the small private most-shameful sentence of his life, said back to him, in another man's voice, without judgment.

Gabriel said —

— *"The embers had been listening, Hestor. The embers had not, in your small private night, spoken. The embers had only recorded. The embers, in the way of embers, do not interrupt. You had, on the morning after, believed the thing you said. You had believed it the way a man believes the first lie he tells about himself before breakfast. You had begun, very quietly, to act as if it were true. You had grown. You had become more. You had become, by the twenty-first year of your reign, the false-god-king of the yellow-stone city — a thing that wears a man and a small god at once, badly, and that has begun, over the years, to consume the small god in order to keep the man going. The small god has been dying for ten years. The small god has been screaming, very softly, in your chest, for ten years, in a frequency only embers can hear. The embers told me. I walked. I have come."*

The king did not open his eyes.

He said, very softly —

— *"Why did you come."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Because the god has been asking. Because the god is, even in its dying, kind — and even now, after ten years of being eaten, the small kind god of Caro would rather that you, who have not been a bad king, were given the gentle ending than the wave. I have come to give you the gentle ending."*

The king opened his eyes.

He said —

— *"What does it cost me."*

Gabriel said —

— *"If you let him out, he will die in the open. If you keep him in, he will die in you. There is no version of this in which he lives. I have come to tell you that. You did not know that. You thought, in the small bad night of your eleventh year, that if you became him you could keep him. You cannot keep him. You can only keep what you are."*

The king said —

— *"Boy."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Hestor."*

The king said —

— *"What do I do."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Let him out."*

The king said —

— *"He will die."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Yes."*

The king said —

— *"I will be only a man."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Yes."*

The king said —

— *"They will overthrow me by spring."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Yes."*

The king said —

— *"You are asking me to die."*

Gabriel said, very gently —

— *"I am asking you to be what you are. The dying is the world's. The dying is not mine to ask of you. I have come only to give you back the word for what you are. The word is Hestor. The word is not god."*

The king wept.

The king wept the way the Nephil at the river had wept. The way Vesta had wept. The way a thing weeps when it has been waiting, in the small private place of itself, for someone to come and name the falseness without pity.

He wept for a long time.

Gabriel did not interrupt.

When the king stopped weeping he said —

— *"How."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Say it."*

The king said —

— *"What."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Say your name. Say only your name. Do not say his name with it. Do not say his name at all."*

The king closed his eyes.

He took a breath that was, the boy could hear, the breath of a man taking off, after fifty years, a small coat that had become too heavy.

The king said, very softly —

— *"Hestor."*

A small fire, on the stone floor in front of the throne — a fire that had not been there a moment before, a fire that came out of the king's mouth on the breath of his own name — flickered into being. The fire was the size of a hand. The fire was warm. The fire did not, in the high cold hall of yellow stone, burn anything. The fire stood in the air for the length of one slow long breath, and the fire said, in a small kind voice that was not the king's —

*thank you, Hestor.*

The fire went out.

The king opened his eyes.

The king was, suddenly, one man, in a way he had not been one man in fifty years. His face — his small ordinary fifty-year-old face — was the face of a man who had been carrying a stone in his pocket for two-thirds of his life, and had not, in carrying it, known it was a stone, and had only, on this morning, set it down, and now was feeling the small surprise of a body that was, after fifty years, light.

The king looked at Gabriel.

The king said —

— *"They will kill me by spring."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Yes."*

The king said —

— *"And I am, after all this, only a man."*

Gabriel said —

— *"A king, still. A man, still. A god, no longer. That is the trade."*

The king nodded.

He said, after a long pause —

— *"What is your name, boy."*

Gabriel did not answer.

He did not, yet, have one.

He had been *child* in his mother's house. He had been *boy* in Otto's. He had been *the namer* in Iossa's small careful angular script. He had been, for fifteen years on the road, simply the small walking thing the embers had been moving across the country. He did not, on the morning of the yellow throne, have a word that was *his*.

He said, very gently —

— *"I do not know it yet."*

The king said —

— *"When you have it, will you come back."*

Gabriel said —

— *"I will be far away. But the embers will tell you, in the small fire in your private chamber at evening, on the day I learn it. You will know."*

The king said —

— *"All right."*

He stood.

He walked, slowly, down the dais.

He stopped in front of Gabriel.

He was, Gabriel saw — for he could see, in the morning light through the high window, things he had not been able to see in the dim of the hall — a small old man. Only that. Fifty. Grey at the temples. Tired around the eyes. A man who had, that morning, lost the thing he had been hiding behind for half his life.

The king knelt.

He knelt the way a small old king kneels — with effort, with the small private surprise of his own legs at being asked to do this.

He took Gabriel's small wrist in both of his hands.

He turned it palm-up. He kissed the inside of Gabriel's wrist, very softly, on the small place the vein ran close to the skin.

He said —

— *"For whatever it has been worth, boy."*

He stood up.

He walked back to the throne.

He sat down.

Gabriel walked back through the hall.

He did not look back.

In the antechamber, Verro was waiting.

Verro did not ask. Verro could see. Verro had heard, through the cracked-open door, every word.

Verro said, simply —

— *"Thank you."*

Gabriel said —

— *"Verro. Open the gate kindly in spring. He has earned, today, the smallness of his ending. Do not, when the cousin comes, let him hurry it."*

Verro said —

— *"I will not."*

He did not.

The cousin, in spring, was let in slowly. The king was killed in his hall, on the yellow throne, in the small still way an old man dies in his own chair. There was no struggle. There was no last word. The king nodded to his cousin. The cousin, who had been expecting a god-king and had found only an old man, was confused, but he did the thing he had come to do, and he did it as kindly as a killing can be done, which is not very, but more so than it might have been.

The cousin would rule for nineteen years.

He would never know why his uncle, in the last six months of his reign, had not fought back.

The cousin would die, much later, a confused old man — and the city, two centuries later, would be forgotten, and the yellow stone would be quarried for other walls in other countries, and the name *Hestor* would not, in the small ordinary memory of any village or town, be remembered.

The embers would remember.

The embers always remembered.

And in a small village in the far south, in the third spring after, on the morning of a small woman's labor in a small wattle-and-daub house at the edge of a small orange grove, a small fire would light itself for one moment on the windowsill above her bed, and she — who had been told by her grandmother of the small god who used to come, in old times, to the hearths of women newly birthing, and who had been told to *welcome him* — would welcome him. She would not be afraid.

The fire would bow.

The fire would go out.

The woman would, that morning, bear a son.

She would name him Hestor.

The small god would live on, in the new boy, in the proper way, and not as a thing in a chair.

That had been the work.

That was what naming was for.

— *Gabriel walked out of the yellow-stone city at noon, with Eshe at his hip and Issa overhead, and he did not look back. The witness-stone he had laid at the gate sang in his chest, that afternoon, only one note — a small clean note of relief. He walked south. He did not weep, that day. He was fifteen. He had not, yet, learned what the second king would cost him.*

— *That would come later.*

— *That would come in the delta.*

— *But on the afternoon of the yellow stone, on the road south, Gabriel walked under a clean blue sky, and the small kind warmth of the released god — Vesta's brother, the small hearth-god of Caro — followed him for a little way at his shoulder, before it dispersed, in the slow way kind gods disperse when they have, at last, been let go, into the wide open warm air of the small ordinary world it had been, for fifty years, wanting only to return to.*
