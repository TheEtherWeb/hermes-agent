# Chapter 10 — A Giant at a Ford

He was six.

He had been walking, by then, for the better part of two years.

He had crossed three rivers — Onerai's, the small swift one in the high pass that froze in winter and roared in spring, and the wide slow one in the country of the olive trees that the embers called *unnamed; do not name her; she has a name you have not learned yet*. He had passed through eleven villages without stopping in any of them long enough to be remembered. He had named, in his slow patient way, the small things along the road — a stretch of bramble that had been waiting to be called *Mother Bramble*, three particular trees that had been waiting to be called by their kinds (*Almond, Plane, Carob*), and one stretch of riverbank that had been waiting only to be looked at and acknowledged, which the embers had told him was, sometimes, all a place wanted.

He had not, in those two years, named another animal.

He had not had to. The animals along his road were already named, in the small ordinary speech of the villages they were near, and the embers had told him *only name the unnamed; do not rename the named, even when the names are small.*

He had named, once, a wind.

It was not the high-pass wind he had been promised. It was a smaller wind, in the lower country, that had been blowing across a particular plateau without any name for the better part of a thousand years and had begun, very quietly, to wish to be called something. He had sat on the plateau for an afternoon. He had listened. The wind had said, in its high thin voice, *I am the wind that bends the grass to the east.* He had said, *Yes.* He had said, *Aren.* The wind had bowed — the grass on the plateau had laid down flat in one long breath, and stayed flat for the length of a held thought, and then risen — and the wind had gone away, satisfied, to bend its grass for the rest of its time.

Eshe was, by then, five years old.

She was, by the small reckoning of sheep, a sheep in her prime. Her wool was thicker than the wool of any sheep the boy had passed in any of the eleven villages. Her bell — Otto had not given her one, but a small bronze bell had attached itself to her collar, somewhere in the high pass, in the way certain small bronze bells attach themselves to certain sheep — was the small sweet sound the boy heard at his heel all day.

She had not, in two years, left him.

She had not eaten a thing she had not eaten in his presence.

She had not, the boy noticed, grown older.

He had not, in his fifth year, thought about this. He thought about it for the first time on the morning of the ford.

The ford was in a stretch of country he had not, yet, walked through. The country was low and wide and the kind of green that comes after a long wet winter. The river at the ford was not deep — it was, by his eye, perhaps to his waist at the middle. The bank on his side was a small sandy slope. The bank on the far side was a small grassy ridge. Between the two banks, the river ran wide and slow over a bed of small smooth stones.

There was a man at the ford.

The man was drinking.

The man was on one knee in the shallow water near the far bank. He was drinking from his cupped hand.

The hand was the size of a clay water-jar.

The boy stopped at the near bank.

He stopped at the small careful distance the embers had taught him to stop at when a thing was *large*. The embers had said, in the long evenings of his walking, *if a thing is large, do not approach until it has set down what it is drinking, what it is eating, what it is holding. large things startle, and a startled large thing does not, anymore, see what is at its feet.*

The boy waited.

Eshe, at his hip, did not press forward.

The man drank.

The drinking — the boy timed it, by counting his own breaths — was a drink, a thing that took two breaths to swallow. The swallowing made the river briefly run shallower at the bend. The boy could see this. The water at the ford, which had been a steady wide flow a moment before, became, at the moment of the man's swallow, a slightly shallower flow. The far bank's grass at the water's edge briefly poked above the surface.

The man set his hand down.

The river rose to its full.

The man, with his hand on the bed of the river, turned his head.

He looked at the boy.

He was nine feet tall.

The boy had known he would be tall. The boy had not, in his six years, known what nine feet looked like. He looked at the man now, and he understood that *tall* was a word that meant different things at different sizes, and that this man — this man on his knees in the shallow water — was *tall* the way a tree was tall and not the way a man was tall. The man's head, even on his knees, was higher than the head of any standing man the boy had seen.

The man stood up.

The standing took, by the boy's careful count, four breaths.

The man, standing, was the size of three of the tallest men the boy had ever seen, stacked one on top of the other.

The man's voice — when he spoke, which he did only after he had finished standing — came up out of his chest the way a drum sounds when struck. It was not loud. It was *complete*. It was the small entire sound of a particular shape of air being moved.

He said —

— *"Child."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

The man waded — and the wading was three of his strides, which would have been twenty of an ordinary man's — to the near bank.

He sat down.

The sitting down took a long time. When he was finished, he was still as tall, seated, as the boy was standing. The boy walked, with Eshe behind him, up to him. The boy stopped at a distance of one of his own paces from the man's knee — which was, the boy understood, the polite distance to which one stood before a sitting Nephil.

He did not yet have the word *Nephil*.

The man did not, yet, have it either.

The man said —

— *"You are alone."*

The boy said —

— *"Eshe."*

The man looked at the sheep.

The man, very gently, smiled.

The man said —

— *"Eshe is a friend."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

The man was quiet for a long time. He had the look of a man — a man, the boy understood, not for human eyes; a man, in the man's own people's reckoning — who had not, in many years, had a conversation, and who had to remember the small motions of the mouth that made speech work.

He said —

— *"What is your name, child."*

The boy did not answer.

He looked at the man.

He looked at the man for a long time.

He had learned, in two years of walking, that one did not name a thing one had not looked at. He looked at the man's face — which was, at full size, the size of the small wattle door of the widow's house. He looked at the man's eyes, which were the color of river stone. He looked at the man's hands, one of which was resting on the boy's side of the bank, palm up, fingers curled, and which was the size of a clay water-jar laid sideways. He looked at the man's hair, which was the dark color of damp earth, and which had not been cut in a long time, and which the wind moved in the small soft way the wind moves any other hair. He looked at the small leather pouch at the man's belt, which was the size of a satchel.

He looked at all of these things, and he listened.

He listened to the man's hands, the way he had listened to the sheep, the way he had listened to the ox's leg. The man's hands said, in the very slow speech of large hands —

*we have done much. we have grown much. we have been called, in the small villages, monster. we have not, in the lifetime of the one who carries us, been called what we are.*

The boy said, in his inward speech —

*what are you.*

The man's hands said —

*the embers know. ask the embers.*

The boy turned, very slightly, his inward ear to the embers.

He had been carrying them, by then, for the better part of six years. He had not, ever, *interrupted* them. They had been a small constant susurration at the back of his attention, telling him a great many things in the slow patient way they told him things. He had learned, in two years, the small art of asking.

He asked.

The embers said, very gently —

*his kind came down from the heights, long ago. they loved the women of the plain. they had children. the children were neither one thing nor the other. they were a kind. the kind has been dying out. no one has named the kind in the man's hearing in his eighty-four years. you have not been told the word for them, child, because the word was never spoken over a hearth — they did not have hearths. they had high places. but the embers have other ways of knowing. we know the word. it is your word now. take it.*

The boy took it.

He turned back to the man.

He said —

— *"Nephil."*

The man's face did not change.

The man's eyes changed.

The man's eyes had been the color of river stone. The man's eyes, on the word *Nephil,* became the color of river stone *that has remembered being a mountain.* The man, who had not in his eighty-four years known what he was — for the things his kind was had been many, and the names the kind had been called had been many, and no one had named the kind in the man's hearing in a way that had *settled* — settled.

The man said, very softly —

— *"That is what we are."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

The man said —

— *"You knew this."*

The boy said —

— *"The embers know."*

The man bowed his head.

The man's bow was not the bow of a giant to a child.

The man's bow was the bow of a being who had not, in eighty-four years, been told what he was by anyone who had the right to tell him.

The man said —

— *"Walk with me a way, child."*

The boy said —

— *"A way."*

They walked.

They walked along the river for three days.

The man did not give the boy his own name. The boy did not ask. The boy had begun, in two years of walking, to understand that *names should not be given unasked*, and that the great courtesy one offered a being who had just been told what kind it was was to leave the lesser names — the names within the kind — for the being to keep or give away as it chose.

The Nephil told the boy, in those three days, of his kind.

He told the boy of the heights.

He told the boy of the long-ago, when the kind had come down — for love, for curiosity, for the small ordinary reasons large beings sometimes have to come into the small ordinary world of smaller beings. He told the boy of the women of the plain, and of the children, and of how the children had not been able to find chairs to sit in at either table. He told the boy that there had been four great Nephil kinds. He told the boy that, in his eighty-four years of wandering, he had met seven other Nephilim, and that all seven had been older than him, and that all seven were, by now, dead.

He told the boy that the kind would, in two or three more generations, be gone.

He said —

— *"We are dying out, child."*

The boy said —

— *"The embers have said so."*

The man said —

— *"Will you tell us, when we are gone, what we were."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

The man said —

— *"Promise me."*

The boy said —

— *"I promise."*

The promise, in the speech of the embers, was a pact, and a pact in that speech was not a word; a pact was a binding. The river, as the boy spoke the word *promise,* slowed at the bend where they were walking, and the slowing was the river's witness, and the river had not, in the boy's six years of being alive, refused to witness a thing he had spoken.

The man wept.

The man wept the way a thing weeps when it has been waiting eighty-four years to be promised.

On the morning of the fourth day, the man bowed to the boy once more, and turned, and walked north up the river toward the highlands his kind kept.

The boy did not see him again.

He kept the promise. He has kept it ever since.

Every Gallant book that has ever been written contains, somewhere in its margins, a small line about the kind that came down. The line, in the oldest version — written, much later, by a Gallant who had heard it from a Gallant who had heard it from Godfrey himself, on the night of his squire-bonding — reads:

> *They were called Nephil, and they were loved, and the world is poorer for the silence of their walking.*

The man at the river was the first of nine Nephilim the boy would meet in the years of his walking.

He had been the boy's first.

The boy stood, on the morning of the fourth day, at the bend where the Nephil had turned north, and he watched the man walk into the small distance and become the size of a tree, and then the size of a man, and then the size of a thing that was no longer the size of anything but a small dark blur on the green of the upper country.

Eshe pressed against his hip.

He said, to Eshe —

— *"He was lonely."*

Eshe said —

*yes.*

He said —

— *"He had been lonely for eighty-four years."*

Eshe said —

*yes.*

He said —

— *"Will I be lonely for eighty-four years?"*

Eshe said, in the small simple speech of sheep that the boy had learned to listen to —

*not as long as I am here.*

He put his small hand in her wool.

He stood for a while longer.

Then he turned, and they walked east.

The morning was still coming from the east.

It was, the boy had begun to suspect, the only direction the morning ever came from — but he kept walking toward it anyway, because it was the only direction worth walking toward, and because the embers, in his chest, had not, ever, told him to turn back.
