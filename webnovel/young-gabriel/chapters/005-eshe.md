# Chapter 5 — Eshe

Otto, two doors down, had eleven sheep.

Otto was a man of the small ordinary sort the village made by the dozen — broad-shouldered, slow-spoken, with a face the color of old leather and hands that had, in their thirty-eight years, done every kind of work the village had to offer and not done any of them especially well. Otto had not chosen to be a shepherd. Otto had, in fact, wanted to be a stoneworker, because the stoneworker's daughter — the widow, now, two doors down — had been the prettiest girl in the village when Otto was nineteen, and Otto had thought, in the small private way of nineteen-year-old men, that if he became a stoneworker he might become the kind of man the stoneworker's daughter would marry.

The stoneworker had died.

The widow had not, after the burial, been the kind of widow who would have looked at a stoneworker's apprentice anyway.

Otto had become a shepherd because his father had been a shepherd, and his father's father, and because the eleven sheep in his small pen behind his small house had been, of all the things his father had left him, the only thing his father had asked him to keep.

Otto did not love his sheep.

He did not hate them, either. He did the small ordinary work a shepherd did — he walked them out in the morning, he walked them in at evening, he sheared them in the spring, he helped them lamb in the winter, and twice a year he sold a wether to the traders who came up from the lower country.

The eleven sheep in his pen were all of them, to Otto, *sheep*. They were not individuals. They were not friends. They were not, in any way he had ever thought about, distinct from one another. The eleven sheep had been born — five of them in last year's lambing, four in the year before, two old ewes who had outlived their useful years and whom Otto had been meaning, for two seasons, to sell — and Otto had not, in any of their births, given any of them a name.

The boy was thirteen months old.

The boy had begun to walk.

The boy walked in the small wobbling way thirteen-month-olds walked — with his hands held a little out from his sides, his small bare feet very deliberate on the dirt of the lane, his attention not on the walking but on the place he was walking *toward*.

The boy walked, on the morning of the seventh day of his thirteenth month, two doors down to Otto's pen.

Otto's pen was a small wattle enclosure behind his house, in which the eleven sheep stood in the morning, waiting to be led out. Otto was inside the house, finishing his small ordinary morning meal of bread and goat's milk. The widow, who had been sweeping the lane in front of her own door, looked up and saw her son walking, with the unhurried gait of a man on an errand, past Otto's door and around the side of Otto's house, and she did not call him back.

She had stopped, in the previous month, calling him back.

She had learned that when he walked toward a thing, the thing was, in some way she did not yet understand, *his to walk toward*. She had learned this from the small piece of straw he had picked up in front of the hearth on the morning Iossa had brought the oak, and from the small carved spoon he had walked, three weeks ago, to the carver's spoon-shelf and picked up and brought home, and from the small grey kitten — born to the woman across the lane's cat, and not, technically, anyone's kitten yet — that he had walked to and lifted by its scruff and laid against his chest, and that the woman across the lane had, when she had seen the boy come back with her kitten, only said *keep him*, as if the kitten had always been the boy's.

The kitten was now, in the small house, called *Quiet*.

*Quiet* did not, anymore, mew.

She let him walk.

She watched.

The boy walked around the side of Otto's house and disappeared.

She set the broom down.

She walked, in her bare feet, after him.

The boy was standing in front of Otto's pen.

Eleven sheep were standing in the pen. They had been, until the boy arrived, doing the small ordinary morning thing that sheep do in a pen — pressing against the wattle, lipping at the corner where a small stalk of grass had grown through, and occasionally bumping each other in the patient way sheep have of asking *are you there. yes. good.*

When the boy arrived, they stopped.

All eleven of them turned their heads.

All eleven of them looked at him.

The widow, behind the boy, did not move.

She had not, in her thirty-one years, seen sheep look at anything the way Otto's sheep were, in that moment, looking at her son. Sheep did not, in her experience, *look* at things. Sheep glanced, sheep grazed, sheep startled, sheep turned away. Sheep did not look at a child the way these eleven sheep were looking at her child — with the small undivided attention of eleven small witnesses who had been told, in some way she could not hear, that a thing was about to be named.

The boy did not look at the eleven.

The boy looked at one.

The one was, to the widow's eye, no different from the other ten. She was a young ewe — not from the previous lambing, but the one before — with cream-colored wool that had been freshly oiled in the spring shearing, and a small white blaze on her forehead, and a small bell around her neck that Otto had put there because the bell had been his father's and his father had liked the sound of it on a sheep he had been fond of.

The boy walked into the pen.

The wattle gate had not been latched. He pushed it open with his small hand.

He walked, with his unhurried walk, to the ewe.

The ewe did not move.

The other ten stepped back. They stepped back, all of them, by half a pace — not afraid, only making room, the way the elders of a village make room in a small house for a man who has come to perform a marriage.

The boy stopped in front of the ewe.

He put his small hand on her wool.

The ewe lowered her head.

The ewe — the widow watched this very carefully, because she would, for the rest of her life, want to remember it exactly — pressed her face against the boy's belly. She pressed her face against him the way a dog presses its face into the lap of someone it has been waiting for.

She said, in the small breathing speech of sheep —

The widow could not hear this. The widow had never been able to hear, in the speech of any animal, anything but the small ordinary sounds animals made.

But the boy could.

The boy heard the ewe say —

*you found me.*

The boy heard her say —

*yes. that is me. that is me.*

Behind the widow, Otto came out of his house.

Otto had heard, through the small back window of his house, the small dry sound of his pen gate opening. He had thought, at first, that one of his sheep had pushed the gate. He had taken his shepherd's stick from beside the door, and he had come out, and he had walked around the side of his house, and he had stopped.

His pen was open.

A small boy was inside his pen, with his hand on the wool of one of his sheep.

Ten of his other sheep had stepped back into a small respectful half-circle around the boy and the one.

The widow was standing in his lane, watching.

Otto, in his thirty-eight years, had not seen anything he had less of a word for.

He said —

— *"Boy."*

The boy did not turn.

Otto said —

— *"Boy. What you done."*

The boy looked up at the ewe.

He said, very gently —

— *"Eshe."*

The ewe lifted her head.

The ewe looked at Otto.

Otto, who had not, in his thirty-eight years, been looked at by any of his sheep, was looked at by this one.

The ewe — *Eshe* — looked at Otto the way a person looks at a man who has been their employer for a long time and has now, in the small ordinary morning of an ordinary day, been told that they are leaving.

The look said —

*goodbye, Otto.*

The look said —

*thank you for the bell.*

The look said —

*I am his now.*

Otto would tell this story, for the rest of his life — and his life was a long one, sixty-three more years — and he would tell it the same way each time. He would tell it like this:

*The boy named her, and the sheep agreed, and from that morning forward, that sheep was no longer my sheep. That sheep was her own sheep. She came when I called her by the name, and she did not come when I called her in the old way. She was sweeter to her lambs. She was meaner to my dog. She lived seventeen years, which is twice the years a sheep should live, and when she died she went into the ground at the foot of the fig tree, because the boy had asked us to put her there, and the boy did not ask us things often, and when the boy asked us a thing, by then, we did the thing.*

In the morning, the morning Otto would tell about, he did not say any of that.

He said, only, to the widow —

— *"He named one of mine."*

The widow said, very softly —

— *"Yes."*

Otto said —

— *"What do I do."*

The widow said —

— *"You let her go."*

Otto looked at the ewe.

The ewe — *Eshe* — was standing very still beside the boy, with the small contentment of a thing that had been, until that morning, only one of eleven, and was now, in the small new grammar of the world, the only one of herself.

Otto walked to the ewe.

Otto knelt — in his thirty-eight years, he had not knelt to a sheep, but he knelt — and he took the small bell from around her neck, because the bell had been his father's and the bell did not belong to her now, it belonged to whoever Otto would, in his old age, decide to give it to.

He did not, in the next sixty-three years, give it to anyone.

He kept the bell on a small peg by his door for the rest of his life, and he rang it once, every morning, when he opened the door to his pen.

In the morning Otto would also, after taking the bell off, take from his belt a small length of new leather he had been planning to cut into a strap for his own sheep-stick. He would cut it, instead, into a small collar. He would oil the collar. He would put the collar around Eshe's neck.

He would say, to the boy —

— *"For when you walk."*

The boy would say, looking up at him with the small undivided attention he had given the embers and the oak —

— *"Otto."*

Otto would say —

— *"Yes."*

The boy would say —

— *"Thank you."*

It was the first time the boy had said *thank you*.

Otto would, walking back into his house with ten sheep instead of eleven, weep — though he would not, in the sixty-three years afterward, tell that part of the story to anyone, because the weeping had not been a sad weeping. It had been the weeping of a man who had been holding, for thirty-eight years, a thing that had not been his, and had finally been given the small kind permission to set it down.

The widow led her son back to her own house.

Eshe walked behind them.

Iossa, that evening, would carve the first name on the panel — *ESHE* — and the boy would put his small hand on the carving and would say, very gently —

— *"Yes."*

The widow would, watching, finally understand what the panel was for.

The panel was a book.

The book was a book of names.

The book had, that evening, one entry.

The book would have, by the end of Iossa's life, seventeen.

The book would, much later — long after the widow's death and Otto's death and Iossa's death and the death of every other person in the village whose name was on it — sit in a museum that no one in the village would ever live to see. The names on it would still be legible. The first name, in a small careful angular script the old man had invented on the spot because the village did not yet have writing, would be —

*ESHE.*

And the small leather collar Otto had made for her, much loved and much repaired, would still be around her neck on the morning, four winters from this one, when she walked out of the village beside the boy and did not, the way the boy did not, look back.
