# Chapter 16 — The Out-of-Season Peach

He walked toward the fire for the rest of that afternoon.

The plain was wider than he had thought. The fire, in the distance, did not move — but the boy walked, and the fire did not get larger as quickly as it should have, and he understood, halfway through the afternoon, that the fire was farther away than it looked, because the fire was *taller* than anything he had judged distances against.

It was the height of a tall man.

It was, when he came at last to within a stone's throw, taller still — perhaps the height of a man and a half.

It burned in the open.

There was no wood at its base. The grass under it was not scorched. The orange of the fire's body did not have, at its bottom, the small black ring of a fire that had been eating a fuel. The fire was, simply, *there* — standing in the air, a tall orange shape, doing the small steady breathing-motion every fire did.

The boy stopped at the edge of the firelight.

He had not, in his eleven years, been afraid of any fire — every fire he had been near had been an ember in another room, and he had been the boy the embers were waiting for. He was not afraid of this one either. He was, instead, attentive in the way the embers had taught him to be attentive when a thing was *important*. He sat down. He folded his small legs under him. He set his hands on his knees.

The woman was sitting on the other side.

The woman was, on first sight, ordinary.

She had black hair to her hips. She wore the small linen of the southern plain — a long open-collared dress of pale undyed cloth, with a small wool wrap over her shoulders against the cold. She was barefoot. She was eating a peach.

The peach was out of season.

The boy had not seen a peach in four months. The peach trees of the upper country bore in late autumn, and by the deep winter of the southern plain, no peach had been on any branch in any of the villages he had passed in two months. The peach in the woman's hand was, by the small ordinary speech of the world, impossible. It was full. It was ripe. The juice on her thumb was the small bright juice of a peach that had been picked, perhaps, that morning.

She looked up.

She said, in the small ordinary voice of any woman of the southern plain —

— *"Boy."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She said —

— *"I have been waiting for you for nine years."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She said —

— *"You know what I am."*

He did not answer.

He looked at her.

He looked at her for a long time.

He looked at her face. He looked at her hands — the one holding the peach, the other resting in her lap. He looked at her hair. He looked at her feet on the winter grass — bare, the grass under them green where the grass three paces away was the brown of dead winter. He looked at the small empty space at her side where, the embers had taught him, a person of her kind kept their *absence* — the small pocket of unbelonging that demigods always wore beside them, the small invisible weight of *I am not from this country and not from any other*.

He listened.

He listened to the absence.

The absence said, in a voice that was not quite the voice of speech, but the voice of a thing that had been alone so long it had begun to *resemble* speech —

*i am two hundred and seven years old. i am the daughter of a great power and a small power. the great power was a god of the high heaven. the small power was a woman who kept a fire in a small village outside the country called Mer. they loved each other for one season. i was born in the autumn after. my mother named me only *daughter*. she did not know what else to call me. she did not have the word. i have not, in two hundred and seven years, had the word. i am of two countries. i am of neither. i do not have a chair to sit in at either table. i have been walking. i have been keeping fires in small places. i have been weeping by myself in the small inner room of my own keeping. i have, every twenty years, come to this plain in winter, and lit this fire that needs no wood, and sat by it, in the hope that the namer would come. for nine years now, the embers have been telling me that the namer was alive. for nine years i have been sitting by this fire every winter, waiting. you are very small. you are eleven. i had hoped you would be older.*

The boy said, very gently —

— *"Half-borne."*

She set the peach down.

She said, in the speech of speech this time —

— *"That is the kindest word for it I have heard."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She said —

— *"Most call me bastard."*

He said —

— *"That is not your name."*

She said —

— *"What is."*

He said —

— *"You have not been given one yet."*

She said —

— *"I have been alive for two hundred and seven years."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She said —

— *"And I have not been given a name."*

He said —

— *"No."*

She put her face in her hands.

She wept.

The boy waited.

He waited the way he had waited for the embers in his ninth month and seventeenth day: without hurry, with no thought of the next thing, with the small undivided attention of a child who had not yet, at eleven, learned that attention could be lent in pieces.

When she stopped weeping she said —

— *"Will you give me one."*

He said —

— *"I came here for that."*

She looked up.

She said —

— *"Now."*

He said —

— *"When you have told me what you wish to be."*

She thought.

She thought, by the small reckoning of the fire and the way the fire bent toward her — for it did, very faintly, lean, the way a fire leans toward a hand held out to it — perhaps three hours.

She said, finally —

— *"I wish to be the one who keeps the small fires."*

He said —

— *"The small fires."*

She said —

— *"The ones that should not go out. The hearths of women who are alone. The lamps of children who are afraid in the dark. The candles in shrines no one tends anymore. Those."*

He nodded.

He sat with the wish for a long time.

He listened to it.

He listened to it the way he had listened to the pipe on Maren's wall. He listened to it the way he had listened to the ox's leg. He listened to it the way the embers had been teaching him, in eleven years, to listen — with the whole of him, with the small undivided attention of a child who had been *given*, on the morning of his birth, an attention that did not break.

He heard the wish.

He heard the kind of name the wish wanted.

He said —

— *"Vesta."*

The fire between them brightened.

The plain around them, which had been winter-grey, gathered for one second a small private warmth.

She said —

— *"That is my name."*

He said —

— *"Yes."*

She put her hand against the fire.

The fire did not burn her. The fire bowed — the orange of its body curved, very slightly, toward her hand, in the small particular bow a flame makes when its true keeper has at last laid her hand against it.

She stood.

She kissed the top of his head, very lightly — and the kiss was warm, and the warmth of the kiss was the warmth of a small hearth on a winter evening, the warmth a child remembers when the child is far from home — and then she walked away across the plain in the direction of every hearth in the world that had been waiting, for two hundred and seven years, for the name of the one who was coming.

She did not look back.

He did not call after her.

He watched her until she was the size of his thumb, and then the size of a small dark spot, and then was, in the winter grey of the plain, gone.

The fire in front of him kept burning.

He looked at it.

He said, to it —

— *"Will you go out, now."*

The fire said, in the small kind voice that was not the voice of any one fire but the voice of every small fire ever kept by a lonely hand —

*no, child. not yet. i will burn here for a great many years. travelers will come. they will warm themselves. they will sleep at my edge. they will not know who lit me. i will, by my burning, hold the place open until the next one comes who needs a keeper. vesta has many small fires to find. this one is mine to hold for her until she returns.*

The boy nodded.

He took, from his hip-bag, the small pinch of salt that was always at the top of the bag.

He flung the pinch into the fire.

The fire took it.

The fire said —

*thank you.*

The boy said —

*you are welcome.*

He stood up.

His legs, which had been folded under him for the better part of four hours, ached. He stretched them.

Eshe, behind him — she had not come into the firelight, the boy now noticed; she had stayed at the edge — pressed against his hip.

She said, in her sheep way —

*was she kind.*

The boy said —

*yes.*

Eshe said —

*was she sad.*

The boy said —

*she had been. she will not be, anymore.*

Eshe said —

*good.*

The boy looked at the fire one more time.

He turned.

He walked.

Eshe walked behind him.

He walked south for half a day — the embers, in his chest, did not, that afternoon, tell him to go any particular direction; they only said *walk, child, find a small shelter for the night, eat. you have done a thing*. He found, at evening, a small rocky outcrop on the plain that had a small dry hollow at its base. He built a small fire of the dry winter grass he could find. He ate, from his hip-bag, a small piece of the bread the last village had given him.

He sat at the small fire.

He looked at it for a long time.

The small fire — the boy now understood, in a way he had not, before — was a *child* of the larger fire he had left on the plain. Every small fire was a child of every other. They were all of them, in the long slow way of fire, family. They had been, for as long as there had been fire, a family — and now, in the cold winter dusk of the southern plain, they had a *keeper*.

He said, to the small fire —

— *"You will not be alone, anymore."*

The small fire — the embers in it — said, very gently —

*we have not been alone in eleven years. you are with us. but it is good, also, that vesta will keep us. yes. it is good.*

He lay down.

Eshe lay against his back.

He slept.

In the morning, he did not look for the great fire of the plain. He did not need to. He could feel, in his chest, that it was still burning. He could feel, also — very faintly, like a small new note in the chord of the witness-stones — Vesta. He could feel her going west, away from him, walking at the small steady pace of a woman with two hundred years' work to do and not, anymore, any reason to hurry.

He stood.

He walked south.

He had, in the small private place of him, the small new warm feeling of a boy who had done a thing he had been told he was for, and had done it well, and had been, in the doing, kissed.

He was eleven years old.

He had named his first demigod.

He had forty more names on the tablet in his tunic.

The embers said, in his chest —

*good, child. now we go to the kings. that is the next work. you will meet the first one in your fifteenth year. you have, before then, four more years of small work. begin.*

He began.

He began again.

Eshe walked behind him.

The morning came from the south.
