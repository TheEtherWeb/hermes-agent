# Chapter 13 — The Bandits

There were three of them.

They had been waiting in a small stand of pines at the bend of the road. They had not, the boy understood as soon as he came around the bend, been waiting for *him* — they had been waiting for anyone, and he had been the first thing the road had brought them in two days. They were the kind of men who had not, in those two days, eaten.

They had knives.

One had two.

The boy stopped at a polite distance.

He had been walking since dawn. The road, that day, was a wide good road in the rolling country that lay between the north plain and the southern lakes, and the road had been empty in the small ordinary way roads in that country were empty in the early afternoon. He had not been thinking about bandits. He had been thinking, instead, about the small singing he had heard in his chest — the third witness-stone had spoken, that morning, from a place six days behind him, with the small particular news that a child had been born in the wayhouse where Tair was, and that the child had been named *for the boy who built the fire when the wayhouse was empty*, even though that boy had had, on the night he had built it, no name to give. He had been thinking about this. He had not been watching the road.

The three men had come out of the pines.

The first man — the one with two knives — said, in the small tired sneering voice men used when they were trying to be larger than they were —

— *"Boy."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

The first man said —

— *"What you got."*

The boy said —

— *"A small wrap of cheese. A handful of dried figs. A small pouch of salt. The wool I am wearing. The sandals on my feet. A small piece of cloth that my mother gave me. A sheep."*

The first man laughed.

The second man, who had only one knife but was the largest of the three, said —

— *"The sheep."*

The boy did not, in the small way some boys would have, step in front of Eshe.

He did not, in the small way some other boys would have, run.

He stood. He looked at the three of them. He listened.

He listened with the long patient ear the embers had given him.

He heard, in the three men, what he had heard in Onerai at the raft, and in the trader with the lame ox, and in the small old woman on the threshold of the village he had passed through two weeks ago who had asked him, with a face that had not, in many years, been hopeful, whether he had seen, on his road, a tall young man with a limp.

He heard, in the three men, fear.

He heard, in the first man, the small private fear of a man who had wanted to be a baker and had not had a father who could pay for the apprenticeship.

He heard, in the second man, the older fear of a man who had been beaten as a child until he had grown larger than the man beating him and had not, since, known what to do with his largeness.

He heard, in the third man — the smallest, the youngest, the one who had not spoken — the small clean fear of a boy of perhaps fifteen who had fallen in with the first two two months ago and had not, since, been able to find a way to leave.

The third was not a bandit yet.

The third was a boy who was, on this afternoon, about to become one if the boy did not, in the small careful next minute, do something.

The boy said, very gently, to the third man —

— *"You do not want to be here."*

The third man — boy, the boy saw, only nine or ten years older than he was — flinched.

The first man said —

— *"What you say, runt."*

The boy did not look at the first man.

He kept his eyes on the third.

He said, to the third —

— *"You can walk. You can walk south, today, on this road. There is a small village four hours south. The smith there needs an apprentice. He had one. The one died last spring of a fever. The smith has not, since, taken another. He will take you. You will eat tonight. You will sleep, tonight, on a clean pallet for the first time in two months. You will be able to walk back to your mother, in five years, with a trade in your hands. You will not, then, owe anyone. Your mother is alive. She is in the village called Ostre. She has been waiting six months for you to come home."*

The third man's face was, by then, the color of bone.

He said, in a small voice —

— *"How."*

The boy said —

— *"You know how I know."*

The third man — boy — looked at the boy.

He looked at Eshe.

He took one step back.

The first man said, sharply —

— *"Where you goin'."*

The third man said —

— *"South."*

The first man said —

— *"You're not goin'."*

The third man — and the boy watched this with the small careful attention he had given the ox's leg, and the river's bend, and the saddle of the high pass — turned. He turned without saying anything else. He walked, in a small steady walk, away from the first two men, down the road south. He did not look back. He had been waiting, the boy understood, two months for permission, and the boy had given it, and he was taking it, and he would not, in his life, look back.

The first man said, then, to the boy —

— *"You. You little shit."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

The first man came at him with the knives.

The boy did not, in the small way the village children of his fifth year would have, run.

He sat down.

He sat down on the road. He sat down with his small hands on his knees, in the small particular sitting the embers had taught him at the back of the widow's hearth in his ninth month and seventeenth day.

He looked at the first man.

He did not, at the first man, *name* anything.

He listened.

He listened with the whole of him — the way he had listened to the embers, the way he had listened to Eshe's wool on the morning of the first true naming, the way he had listened to Aram in the saddle.

He listened to the first man's fear.

The first man — *Mauro* was his name, though the boy did not need to know it — had a fear at the bottom of him. It was the small fear that he was, in the end, not the man his father had told him to be. He had not been a baker. He had not been a soldier. He had not been a son who came home with a wife. He had been, in the end, only this — a man with two knives, in a stand of pines, waiting for whoever the road would bring. He had, in his thirty-one years, been ashamed of himself for so long that the shame had become the *shape* of him, and the only way he knew, now, to stop feeling the shame was to make someone else feel a fear larger than his.

The boy looked at him.

The boy did not name the fear.

The boy said, very gently —

— *"Mauro."*

The first man stopped.

He stopped midstride, two paces from the boy.

He stared.

He said —

— *"How."*

The boy said —

— *"I have not named anything in you. I have only used the name your mother gave you. I am not going to name you. The thing I could name in you, you would not, today, want named — and I do not think it is mine to name. But I will tell you something. Are you listening."*

Mauro said, in a voice that was not, anymore, the sneering voice —

— *"Yes."*

The boy said —

— *"There is a small old woman who runs a bakery in a town five days east. Her name is Anna. Her husband died last summer. She has been unable, since, to lift the heavy sacks of flour from the back of the cart to the bakery floor. She has been turning customers away. She has been, for a year, the kind of lonely a woman is when she has lost the man she lifted things with. If you walk five days east on this road, and you ask in the town for the bakery, and you tell Anna you are looking for work, she will give you work. She will give you a room above the bakery. She will, in three years, teach you to bake. She will, in five years, give you the bakery, because she will, by then, be too tired to run it, and you will, by then, have become the man who can be trusted with it. You will become a baker, Mauro. You will become the man your father wanted you to be — but in a different town than he thought, and after a long road he would not have approved of. Do you understand."*

Mauro stood very still.

He said, in the same small voice —

— *"Why."*

The boy said —

— *"Because the road brings people. Because I am the road today. Because you can put down the knives, or you can use them on me, and using them on me will not, in the end, do for you the thing using them is supposed to do. I have nothing worth taking. The salt is small. The cheese is older than you think. Eshe is not the sheep you think she is. You will, if you take her, find that you cannot kill her, and you will, in the failing, become the kind of man who knows he tried to kill a thing he should not have tried to kill, and you will not, after that, be able to walk to Anna. That is what I am offering you, Mauro. Use the knives, and lose Anna. Set them down, and find her."*

Mauro looked at the knives in his hands.

He looked at the second man.

The second man — the largest of the three, who had been standing the whole time with his single knife at his side — said, in a small low voice that was not a sneering voice either —

— *"Boy."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

The second man said —

— *"What about me."*

The boy looked at him for a long time.

He said, very gently —

— *"There is, ten days east of here, a small holy place. It is a shrine, very old, very small. The shrine has had no keeper for two generations. The shrine wants a keeper. The keeper does not have to be a holy man. The keeper has only to sweep the floor in the morning, and clean the small bowl at evening, and listen to the people who come. People come to the shrine to say things they cannot say at home. The shrine has not, in two generations, had a man who would listen. You would listen. You have been waiting your whole life for someone to tell you it was all right to listen instead of to hit. I am telling you. Go to the shrine. The shrine is in the country of olives, in the small village called Caro. You will find it. The shrine will know you when you come. You will be its keeper for forty years. You will, in those forty years, hear so much sorrow that you will not, anymore, need to make any of your own. You will die of an old age. You will be buried in the shrine yard. No one in this stand of pines, today, will remember you. That is the trade. Take it or leave it."*

The second man set his knife down on the ground.

He did not say anything else.

He turned. He walked east. He did not look back.

Mauro, on the road, was crying.

He was not the kind of man who cried.

He set both knives down on the road.

He said —

— *"Boy."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

Mauro said —

— *"My ma."*

The boy said —

— *"Anna is not your ma. But she will be like a ma. She has been waiting for someone she can teach. You have a clean ma in a village three days north of where she is. You can go to her after, when you are a baker. She will not, then, be ashamed."*

Mauro nodded.

He turned. He walked east — the same direction as the second man, but at a small careful distance behind, because they were not, the boy understood, going to walk together. They were going to two different lives.

The boy sat on the road for a while.

Eshe walked up to him. She put her face against his shoulder.

He said —

— *"Eshe."*

She said —

*yes.*

He said —

— *"I did not name them."*

She said —

*no.*

He said —

— *"I sent them. I told them what they were going to be. Was that —"*

He did not finish the sentence.

The embers, in his chest, finished it for him.

*— allowed.*

The embers said, very gently —

*yes, child. it is a kind of naming. you did not put a word on them; you put a road in front of them. that is allowed. that is, in fact, the kind of naming the gallants will do, after you. you have, today, taught yourself a thing the gallants will need. a road can be named for a man. you put the road on his foot. you do not put the name on his head. it is a softer naming. it works. it works for ordinary men, who do not, like the river and the wind and the sheep, want to be named in the old way. you have invented a thing, today, child. write it down.*

The boy did not, that evening, write it down. He did not yet, at nine, write very much. He would, in his fifteenth year, write down what he had done that afternoon — and what he wrote would be the first sentence of the book the Gallants would later call the *Rule of the Road*, the first founding text of the order.

The sentence would read:

> *To a man who does not wish to be named, give a road. The road will name him, in time, in his own hand.*

But that was six years away. That afternoon the boy only put his hand in Eshe's wool, and he sat on the road for a little while longer, and he watched the dust settle on the place the third man had walked away in.

Then he stood.

He walked east.

Eshe walked behind him.

The road was clean.

The pines were quiet.

The embers, in his chest, kept their slow watch.

He walked.
