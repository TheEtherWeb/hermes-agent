# Chapter 8 — The First Road

The road east, in the boy's fifth year, was not yet a road.

It was a track.

The track had been worn by sheep, mostly, in the small slow centuries before the village had been a village — by sheep walking from the upper grazing to the lower, and back, season after season, in the small patient lines sheep walk. After the sheep had made the track, the small ordinary people of the small ordinary villages along it had used it for their small ordinary errands. By the time the boy stepped onto it, on the morning of his leaving, the track was a track of the kind one could walk on with sandalled feet without much pain — packed, in most of its length, by enough walking that the small sharp stones had been pressed into the dirt and the dirt had become almost a floor.

He walked on it.

Eshe walked behind him.

He walked at the unhurried pace of a four-year-old who had been told by no one to hurry, and he walked for, by the small reckoning of the embers in the small fires of the small villages he passed without entering, the first three days.

He did not enter the villages.

The villages were not on his road, in the small particular sense the embers used. They were *near*. They were *nearby*. They were *not for now*. He walked past them. He slept, on the first night, under a hedge — Eshe curled at his back, her wool warmer than any blanket the widow could have woven him. He slept, on the second night, in the small windbreak of a stone wall that some farmer had built and forgotten. He slept, on the third night, inside the dry trunk of a hollow olive tree, which had been waiting, in the patient way of hollow olive trees, for the kind of small traveler who would know to climb in.

He ate, in those three days, very little.

He ate the first fig at the ford of the lower river, where his mother had — through the embers — seen him sit on the afternoon of the morning he left.

He ate the second fig on the morning of the third day, sitting against the trunk of the hollow olive.

He ate the cheese in small careful pieces over the three days, and he gave one piece of it to Eshe, who took it from his hand in the small careful way she had of taking things from him — with her teeth not touching his fingers, even by accident.

He did not, on those three days, get hungry.

He would not, in the years of his walking, get hungry in the way other travelers got hungry. It was not because he was a god. He was not a god. It was because the embers, when he sat in front of them in the evenings, would tell him *do not eat tonight; you have enough for the morning*, and he would not eat, and the morning would come, and he would not be hungry. The embers were good at this. The embers had been keeping bodies alive in their evenings for a very long time.

On the morning of the fourth day, he came to the wagon.

The wagon was at the edge of the track, half a mile from a village he had not stopped at the previous evening. The wagon had two wheels, and a low bed, and a small canvas tent pitched on the bed in the way of a man who slept in his own wagon. There was an ox in the harness. There was a man sitting on the small wood seat of the wagon with his head in his hands.

The ox was lame.

The boy could see this from the road. The ox's left foreleg was held a little off the ground. The ox's whole body was held in the small careful tilt of an animal carrying its weight on three legs instead of four.

The boy walked, with Eshe behind him, up to the wagon.

The man did not look up.

The boy said, in the small ordinary voice of a four-year-old —

— *"Sir."*

The man looked up.

The man was not very old — perhaps twenty-five — and he was very tired. His eyes were red in the way of a man who has not slept. His hands were brown with the small dust of three days on the road. His tunic was a trader's tunic — the long-cut, double-belted tunic of the men who carried goods between the small villages of the upper country and the larger towns of the lower.

The man said —

— *"Boy."*

The boy said —

— *"Your ox is lame."*

The man said —

— *"My ox is lame. I have been sitting here since yesterday morning. I cannot move the wagon. I cannot leave the wagon. I do not know what to do."*

The boy said —

— *"May I look at him."*

The man, in his twenty-five years of trading, had not been asked by a four-year-old whether the four-year-old could look at his ox. The man laughed — a small dry laugh of a man too tired for the laugh to be much. He said —

— *"Look at him, boy. If you can fix him I will give you the wagon."*

The boy walked, with Eshe behind him, to the ox.

The ox lowered its head.

The ox was, the boy could see, an old ox. Its harness had been worn for many years; the leather was the kind of dark soft brown that leather only becomes after fifteen seasons of an animal's warm neck pressing against it. The ox's eyes were the kind of patient, slightly clouded brown of an old work-animal that had, in its long life, pulled a great many wagons through a great many days and was, on this morning, more tired than lame.

The boy put his hand on the ox's foreleg.

The ox did not flinch.

The boy held his hand on the leg for a long time. He was, the embers had taught him, *listening*. He was listening to what the ox's leg wanted to be called.

The ox's leg said, in the small slow speech of the body parts of animals, which was the slowest speech in the world —

*tired.*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

The ox's leg said —

*not broken. not torn. tired. very tired. fifteen years tired. one more day tired.*

The boy said —

*if I name you, you will rest.*

The leg said —

*if you name me, the man will think I am healed. but I will only be rested. you must tell the man.*

The boy said —

*all right.*

He took his hand off the leg.

He looked at the ox.

He said, very gently —

— *"Patient."*

The ox lifted its head.

The ox — *Patient,* now — set its left foreleg down on the ground. Set it down all the way down, in the small careful way an old animal sets down a leg it has been favoring. The leg held. Patient stood, for the first time in two days, on all four legs. Patient shook his harness — once, a long slow shake, the way an old animal shakes when something has settled in him — and was still.

The man, on the wagon seat, did not move.

He had watched.

He had watched the boy with his small hand on the ox's leg, and he had watched the ox lift its head, and he had watched the ox set its leg down. He had not, in the watching, said anything.

He said now —

— *"Boy."*

The boy turned.

The man said —

— *"What did you do."*

The boy said, in the small ordinary voice of a four-year-old —

— *"I named him Patient. He is not healed. He is rested. He will walk for a day. After that, you must let him rest properly. He has been pulling your wagon for fifteen years. He has been very faithful. If you treat him well from today, he will walk for another two years, and then he will need to be let go to graze and not to pull. If you treat him badly, he will walk for one more month and then he will lie down and you will lose him."*

The man got down from the wagon.

The man got down very slowly.

The man walked, in his tired way, to his ox. He put his hand on the ox's neck. The ox lowered its head and pressed it against the man's chest, in the small particular way old work-animals press their heads against the chests of the men who have driven them for a long time.

The man said, to the ox —

— *"Patient."*

The ox did not say *yes*. The ox was not Eshe. The ox was an ox, who had been given his true name by the boy, but the ox would not, in the small particular speech of named things, *speak*. The ox would, instead — and this was the way most named animals were — simply *answer*. The man had said the name. The ox had pressed its head against him. That was the ox's *yes*.

The man wept.

He wept very quietly, with his head against his ox's head. He wept the way Otto had wept, four years before, in the small private back of his own house. He wept the way men wept when they had been holding, for a very long time, a thing they had not let themselves love.

After a while, the man said —

— *"Boy."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

The man said —

— *"What is your name."*

The boy was quiet.

The boy did not, yet, have a name. He had not been given one. He had been *child* in his mother's house. He had been *boy* in Otto's. He had been *the namer* in Iossa's small careful angular script — though not, yet, on the panel; Iossa had refused, in the four years, to carve the boy's name on the panel until the boy gave him one to carve, and the boy had not. He had said, on the evening Iossa had asked, *I do not know it yet. Leave the first line empty. I will come and write it when I know.*

The boy said, to the trader —

— *"I do not have one yet."*

The man said —

— *"How can you not have a name."*

The boy said —

— *"It has not come."*

The man said —

— *"Will it come?"*

The boy said —

— *"Yes. When I have walked far enough."*

The man, in his tired way, nodded. He had walked far enough, in his own twenty-five years, to know what *far enough* meant. He did not, like most men, dispute it.

The man said —

— *"I said I would give you the wagon."*

The boy said —

— *"I do not want the wagon. The wagon is yours."*

The man said —

— *"I owe you."*

The boy said —

— *"Walk Patient slowly. That is what you owe."*

The man said —

— *"Will you not at least take something."*

The boy looked at the wagon. The wagon had, in its small canvas tent, the things a trader carried — small clay jars of oil, lengths of cloth, a few bronze tools, a basket of dried figs from the lower country, a small wooden box of salt.

The boy said —

— *"A handful of salt."*

The man took a handful of salt from the small wooden box and wrapped it in a small square of cloth and tied it with a thread and held it out.

The boy took it.

The boy said —

— *"Thank you."*

The man said —

— *"Where are you going, boy."*

The boy said —

— *"East."*

The man said —

— *"What is east."*

The boy said —

— *"I will find out."*

The man laughed — for the second time that morning, but warmer — and got back up onto his wagon, and clicked his tongue, and Patient lifted his left foreleg and put it down again, and pulled, and the wagon moved.

The wagon moved east.

It moved past the boy.

The man, on the wagon, turned and waved.

The boy waved back.

The man went down the road and was, in a little while, gone.

The boy stood at the side of the road.

Eshe pressed against his hip.

He said, to Eshe —

— *"He was tired."*

Eshe said, in her sheep way —

*yes.*

He said —

— *"The world is full of tired men with old animals who have not been named."*

Eshe said —

*yes.*

He said —

— *"We will name them, when we come to them. We will not look for them. They will be on the road."*

Eshe said —

*yes.*

He sat down on a flat stone at the side of the track.

He took out the small square of cloth with the salt in it.

He set it on the stone beside him.

He took, from inside his tunic, the small witness-stone he had been carrying since the third winter. He set the witness-stone on top of the salt.

He said —

— *"This is the first salt."*

The witness-stone, in the small slow speech of stones, said —

*recorded.*

He picked the witness-stone up. He put it back inside his tunic. He folded the salt back into his hip-bag.

He stood up.

He kept walking.

The road east was, in the boy's fifth year, not yet a road. It would be, by his eighth, beginning to be one. It would be, by his twentieth, the road every wagon in the upper country took to the great middle sea. It would be, by the time the Gallants were old, the road that the old men called *the namer's track*, although no one in the order would, by then, remember why.

The boy did not think about any of that.

The boy walked.

Eshe walked behind him.

The morning kept coming from the east, and they kept walking toward it, and they would, in nine more years, meet a giant at a river, and a woman with a peach on a winter plain, and a false king on a yellow throne — and they would, in twenty-three more years, walk, between them and alone, into a grey that was not a place.

But that morning, on the fourth day of his walking, the boy did not think any of those thoughts.

He thought, only —

— *I will name what comes.*

The embers, in his chest, where they had been since the morning under the fig tree, said, very gently —

*yes, child. yes. begin where you can. begin again.*

He began.

He began again.

He kept beginning, in the small ordinary way the world keeps beginning, every morning he walked.

— *End of Arc 1.*
