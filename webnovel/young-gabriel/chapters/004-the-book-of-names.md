# Chapter 4 — The Book of Names

The old man two ridges south of the village was a carver.

His name, in the village's small ordinary speech, was Iossa. Iossa was sixty-one years old. Iossa had been carving things — spoons, bowls, the long handles for the women's looms, the small clay-soaked rods the men used to mark the boundaries of their grazing — for so long that no one, except his own daughters, could remember a time when Iossa had not been the man you walked to with a piece of wood and a question.

Iossa did not, in the small ordinary way of the village, hear what had happened at the widow's hearth.

Iossa heard it the way Iossa heard most things — late, slowly, and from the wrong person. The wrong person, on the morning of the third day after the boy had said *coal*, was the woman who fetched water from the lower well. She came to Iossa's small house with a length of leather she wanted made into a strap, and she set the leather down, and she said —

— *"You have heard about the boy."*

Iossa said, in his slow way —

— *"Which boy."*

The woman said —

— *"The widow's."*

Iossa looked up.

The woman told him.

Iossa listened. Iossa was very good at listening. Iossa had learned, in sixty-one years, that most of what was said by most people was not the thing the person had come to say, and that the thing the person had come to say usually arrived in the third sentence after the third pause. He waited for the third pause.

The third pause came after the bit about the bowl.

Iossa, in the third pause, said —

— *"A bowl."*

The woman said —

— *"The bowl is the finest bowl in the village. The bowl is the bowl every bowl is now compared to. My bowl is shame to my hand, since."*

Iossa said —

— *"What did he say."*

The woman said —

— *"He said *bowl*."*

Iossa was quiet for a long time.

Iossa, in his sixty-one years, had carved a great many bowls. He had carved his first bowl when he was eight. He had carved his last bowl the previous autumn — a wedding bowl for his middle daughter, who had married a man four valleys away and taken the bowl with her, and Iossa had not, since, felt the wish to carve another. He had thought, in the small private place of him, that he was done with bowls.

He said, to the woman —

— *"Take the leather home. I will make your strap tomorrow."*

The woman said —

— *"I came two ridges. I will not come two ridges again tomorrow."*

Iossa said —

— *"Then take the leather home and come back when you can, but I will not make your strap today."*

The woman left.

Iossa walked, that afternoon, the two ridges to the village.

He did not bring a tool.

He brought, instead, a piece of oak.

The piece of oak was the inside of a panel he had cut out of a fallen tree the previous winter. It was the kind of oak you cut from a tree that has been struck by lightning — dense, dark, slightly burnt at the heart, with the small black scar where the lightning had passed running like a vein through the middle of it. He had been saving the oak. He had not, when he had cut it, known what for. He had only known that the tree had been telling him, in the way trees sometimes tell carvers, *not yet. not yet. someday.*

He had wrapped the oak in cloth.

He had set it on a shelf above his hearth.

He had, on the morning the woman with the leather had come to his door, taken the oak down from the shelf without thinking about it. He had not, in taking it down, decided to bring it to the village. He had only — and this is the part of the story Iossa would tell at every winter gathering for the rest of his life — *taken it down*, the way a man takes down his own coat when the door is opened to a wind he has been expecting.

He walked the two ridges.

He came to the widow's house at the small hour of evening when the lamps were just lit.

He knocked.

The widow opened the door.

The widow was — Iossa had not seen her since the burial of her husband — thinner than he remembered, and quieter, and her eyes were the eyes of a woman who had begun, very recently, to sleep again. He had seen those eyes, before. His own wife had had them, after their first daughter was born.

The widow said —

— *"Iossa."*

Iossa said —

— *"You bore a son."*

The widow said —

— *"I did."*

Iossa said, holding up the wrapped oak —

— *"May I bring this in."*

The widow looked at the oak.

The widow looked at Iossa.

The widow stepped back from the doorway and let him in.

The boy was on the dirt floor in front of the hearth.

The hearth was burning a small evening fire. The coal — the *coal*, the thing the boy had named on the third morning before — was at the back of the hearth, separated from the new fire by a small stone the widow had placed between them. The boy was sitting in front of the new fire. He was holding a small piece of straw. He was turning the piece of straw over and over in his hands, the way a man turns over a thought.

He looked up when Iossa came in.

He looked at Iossa for a long time.

Iossa, who had not been looked at like that in his sixty-one years, did not move.

The boy said —

— *"Old."*

Iossa said —

— *"Yes."*

The boy said —

— *"Carver."*

Iossa said —

— *"Yes."*

The boy looked at the oak in Iossa's hands.

The boy said —

— *"Open."*

Iossa knelt — his knees were not, anymore, the knees of a man who knelt easily, but he knelt — and he set the wrapped oak down on the floor between them, and he opened the cloth.

The oak lay between them.

The boy did not touch it.

The boy looked at it.

The boy looked at the small black scar of the lightning running through the heart of the wood, and the boy looked at Iossa, and the boy said —

— *"Yes."*

Iossa, in his sixty-one years of carving, had never been told *yes* by a piece of wood. He had been told *not yet*. He had been told *not me*. He had been told *another, smaller*. He had been told, once, by a length of cedar, *I am for a coffin; do not make me a bowl*, and he had made a coffin, and the man it had carried had been his own father.

He had never been told *yes*.

The boy said, looking at Iossa —

— *"Write."*

Iossa said —

— *"I do not write."*

The boy said —

— *"You will."*

Iossa was, for a long time, quiet.

He had not, in sixty-one years, written a single word. He could not. The village did not write. The village did not have, in any of its houses, a written word — not on a doorpost, not on a tool-handle, not on a grave-stone. The village kept its memory in the way villages kept memory before there were words written down: in the voices of the old women, who sat by the hearths in the evenings and said the same things in the same order until the small children of the village had learned the order without being taught.

But Iossa, on the dirt floor of the widow's house, took his small carving knife from his belt.

He had not known he had been wearing it.

He looked at the boy.

The boy said —

— *"E."*

Iossa did not know what *E* was.

He held the knife above the oak.

The boy, looking very patiently at him, opened his mouth and made a small shape with it — a small open shape, the shape the mouth makes when it makes the sound *e*. The boy did not make the sound. He only made the shape. He looked at Iossa, and he looked at the oak, and he made the shape again.

Iossa, in his sixty-one years, had carved the shape of every leaf he had ever seen and every face he had ever loved and every small useful thing the village had ever asked him for.

He had not, until that evening, carved a shape that was the shape of a sound.

He set the knife to the oak.

He made the small open shape — the *E* — at the top of the panel.

The oak accepted the cut.

The boy made another shape with his mouth.

Iossa carved it.

The shapes, in order, were —

*E*
*S*
*H*
*E*

The widow, behind them, made a small sound.

She had set her hand on the back of a chair, and the chair was holding her up.

The boy turned, very slightly, to look at her.

The boy said, very gently —

— *"Soon."*

Iossa did not, in that moment, know what *soon* meant. He did not, in fact, know what the four shapes he had just carved meant, either. He only knew that the oak had said *yes*, and that the boy had told him to write, and that the four shapes had been the first thing the boy had asked him to write.

He turned the oak.

He looked at it.

He said, to the boy —

— *"What is *ESHE*."*

The boy said —

— *"You will see."*

Iossa set the oak down. He set the knife on top of it. He stood up — his knees complaining now, the way old knees complain when they have been knelt on for an hour at the order of a child — and he said, to the widow —

— *"I will leave this here. With your permission. The boy will tell you, when he wishes, what to add to it."*

The widow said —

— *"Iossa."*

Iossa said —

— *"Yes."*

The widow said —

— *"Will you come back."*

Iossa said —

— *"I will come back every evening, until the panel is full."*

The widow said —

— *"How long will that be."*

Iossa said, looking at the boy —

— *"That will be the rest of my life."*

The boy nodded.

The boy was almost ten months old.

Iossa walked the two ridges home.

He walked the two ridges back in the morning.

He walked them every morning, and every evening, for the rest of his sixty-one years. He carved seventeen names, in the small angular script the village would, much later, call *the carver's hand* — the first writing the village had ever had, invented one shape at a time on the floor of a widow's house, at the patient direction of a child who was learning, in the same season, to walk.

The first name was *ESHE*.

The second name would not be carved for three months.

The boy was patient.

The oak was patient.

Iossa, in the third month of his daily walk, would understand what *ESHE* was.

And on the morning he understood, Iossa would, for the first time since his middle daughter's wedding, weep.
