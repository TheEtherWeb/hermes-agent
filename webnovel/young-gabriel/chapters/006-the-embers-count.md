# Chapter 6 — The Embers Count

By his second year, every animal in the village had a name.

He did not name them all at once. He named them, the way he named everything, in the patient order the embers gave him. He named Otto's other ten sheep over the course of one autumn. He named the woman across the lane's cat (*Quiet's* mother) on a winter afternoon when the cat had come to sit by the boy's hearth and had looked up at him with the small careful look of a creature who has decided, in her own time, that she is ready. He named the dogs — there were four — in a single morning, by sitting on the threshold of his mother's house and waiting for each one to come to him in turn. They came in turn. They had agreed, in some way the village did not see, who would go first.

By the end of his second year, the wooden panel had fifteen names.

Iossa had carved them all.

The shapes of the village's first letters had, by then, become a small alphabet of their own. Iossa had invented them — invented is the wrong word, the widow thought; *received* was the truer one — one shape at a time, at the patient direction of the boy who sat across from him on the dirt floor and made, with his small mouth, the open and closed shapes of the sounds the names contained.

Iossa, by the end of the second year, could read.

He could not read very much. He could read the fifteen names on the panel, and he could read his own name, which he had carved into the inside of his door-frame one quiet evening because the boy had told him *put yourself in the book; you are in the book*.

Iossa would, in the third year, begin to teach his daughters.

The daughters would, much later, begin to teach the village.

The village would, in fifty years, be a village that knew how to write — the first such village in two hundred miles — and the village would, in its long old age, forget that the writing had begun on the floor of a widow's house at the direction of a child.

But we are still in the boy's second year.

He sat by the hearth, every evening.

He sat by the hearth alone, in the way he had sat in front of it on the morning of *coal* — back straight, hands on his knees, the small undivided attention of a thing that had a great deal to listen to and no use for hurry. His mother, by then, did not interrupt. She would sit at her loom or at her grindstone, and she would do her small ordinary evening work, and the boy would sit by the hearth, and the embers would speak.

The embers, in the boy's second year, began to count.

He did not, the first time they counted, understand what they were doing.

He had asked them, on a particular winter night, what the sounds outside the door were. The sounds outside the door had been the small thin sounds of the wind moving across the ridge above the village. The boy had been listening to them for the length of an hour, and he had asked the embers —

*what is that.*

The embers said —

*wind.*

The boy said —

*one wind.*

The embers said —

*one of three.*

The boy was quiet for a long time. He had not, until that night, known that *wind* was a kind of thing that came in numbers. He had thought *wind* was *wind*, the way *bowl* was *bowl* — one true thing, with one true name.

He said —

*three what.*

The embers said —

*three winds. the wind of the high pass, which is old. the wind of the lower country, which is young. the wind of the sea, which is salt. you will name one of them. the others are not yours.*

The boy said —

*which is mine.*

The embers said —

*the old one. but not yet. you will name him on a high pass, and the high pass is far, and you are still very small. wait.*

The boy waited.

He waited the way the boy waited for everything — without restlessness, without anticipation, with the quiet patience of a thing that had been told *not yet* and had not, in its very young life, ever been told *no*.

But the next night, when he sat in front of the hearth, the embers said —

*ask.*

The boy said —

*what.*

The embers said —

*ask the things. they will tell you what kind they are.*

The boy said —

*the bowl told me. the sheep told me.*

The embers said —

*yes. they told you because you sat with them. sit with the others. they will tell you.*

The boy began to sit.

He sat, in the third winter, in front of every kind of thing he could find.

He sat in front of a piece of bread, which told him it was *bread*, and a piece of olive, which told him it was *olive*, and a piece of cured meat the trader had brought up from the lower country, which told him it was *not for me to name; my name is from far away; ask the trader.* He sat in front of stones. He sat in front of, on three separate afternoons, three separate stones that the widow had brought up from the lower well, and each of the three stones told him a different name. The first stone said *white,* which was the name of the kind of stone, which the boy understood. The second stone said *anchor,* which the boy did not understand, and the embers — when he asked — said *that one pins a place to itself. you will use it later.* The third stone said *witness*, which the boy did not understand either, and the embers said *that one is the kind that remembers what happens near it. you will use that one later too. take it with you when you go.*

The boy took the witness-stone and laid it on the small shelf beside his pallet.

He did not, that evening, name it aloud. He had been told its kind, but he had not, yet, been told its name. Its name would come, the embers said, on the morning he set it down somewhere.

The widow watched him sit in front of stones for an entire winter.

She did not interrupt.

She had, by then, accepted that her son's small ordinary play was not the small ordinary play of other children, and that the other children of the village — when they had, in the previous summer, tried to invite him to throw rocks at a stump with them — had come back to their mothers very quiet, and had said, each of them in their own words —

— *"He is not playing. He is doing something. We did not want to bother him."*

The other children of the village had not, since that summer, tried to invite him to anything.

They were not afraid of him.

They were, instead, *deferential* — a word none of them knew, and none of their mothers had taught them, but a thing they were nonetheless. They walked, when they passed him in the lane, the small ordinary distance they would have walked around an elder. They lowered their small voices, when they spoke near him. They brought, on the small ordinary days when the village's children brought offerings of crab-apples and the small late-summer plums to one another, the smallest and finest plum to the boy's door.

The boy ate the plums.

The boy said, to the children at the door —

— *"Thank you."*

The children went away pleased.

The widow did not, in the second and third winters, see her son speak to another child more than ten or fifteen times. She did not, in those winters, see him afraid, either. He was not lonely. He was, she had begun to understand, the kind of child who carried his own company — and his company was Eshe, who slept on the dirt floor beside his pallet every night, and Quiet the cat, who had taken to riding on his shoulder, and the embers at the back of the hearth, who never ran out of things to tell him.

But the widow, in the fourth winter, began to be afraid.

She did not, the way a mother is afraid of a sickness, name the fear.

She was afraid because she had begun, in the way a mother knows, to feel that her son was *gathering*.

He was gathering. He was gathering the names of the village and the names of the stones around the village and the names of the small useful things in his mother's house. He was filling a small basket of names — though the basket was inside him, and not in any pouch she could see — and one day, when the basket was full, he would, she thought, *take the basket somewhere.*

She did not know where.

She suspected, in the small private place of her, that he did not know where either.

She watched him.

She watched the way he sat by the hearth in the evenings. She watched the way he ate. She watched the way he held Eshe's wool when he and Eshe lay together in the small hour before sleep, with his small hand wound in her wool the way a sailor winds his hand in a rope before a storm. She watched the way he looked at the doorway, sometimes, when the door was open in the afternoons.

He looked east.

He looked east the way a man looks at the place he is going.

The widow began, on the third night of the fourth winter, to dream again of the man with no face.

The man, in the dream, did not say anything new.

The man said, only —

— *"Soon, Eshmun."*

The widow said, in the dream —

— *"How soon."*

The man said —

— *"Spring. After the last frost."*

The widow said —

— *"He is four."*

The man said —

— *"Yes."*

The widow said —

— *"He is too small."*

The man said —

— *"He is exactly the right size. He has been the right size since the morning under the tree. You have not made him smaller in his waiting. You have not made him larger, either. You have done what was asked of you. He is the right size."*

The widow said —

— *"I do not want him to go."*

The man said, very gently —

— *"No one who has ever borne a child has."*

She woke.

She sat up.

The embers, at the back of the hearth, were brighter than usual.

She got up. She walked, in her bare feet, to the hearth. She knelt down.

She said, to the embers —

— *"How long do I have."*

The embers, who had spoken to her on the morning the boy had become *Eshe-mother* and *she who* and *the keeper of the embers*, but who had not, since, said anything she had been awake to hear, said —

*one more season, daughter.*

She put her face in her hands.

She did not weep.

She had wept, in the first three years, very rarely. She did not weep, in the fourth winter, either. She had begun, in the long quiet of her widow's house, to be the kind of woman who did not need weeping to feel what she was feeling.

She said, to the embers —

— *"Will you go with him."*

The embers said —

*we are everywhere there is a hearth. we will be with him every night.*

She said —

— *"Will you bring him home."*

The embers were quiet for a long time.

The embers said, very gently —

*we will bring him home twice. and the third time, daughter, he will bring himself home. but you will not see him on the third time. you will be the home he is bringing himself to.*

She closed her eyes.

She nodded.

She said —

— *"I see."*

She got up from the hearth.

She walked, in her bare feet, back to her pallet.

She lay down beside her son.

She put her hand on his small chest. He was, in the way of small children, asleep with the small steady breath of a thing that had no fear of the morning. She watched him breathe.

She said, very quietly, into the dark —

— *"You are mine. Until the morning. You are mine."*

The boy, in his sleep, did not answer.

But the embers, at the back of the hearth, brightened — once, slowly, the way a thing brightens when it has heard something it agrees with — and then went down to their small patient orange glow again, the way a thing settles back to its watching when it has, for a moment, been allowed to speak.

The boy was four years old.

There were ninety-one days left to the morning he would leave.

The widow began, the next day, to weave him a small wool tunic for the road.
