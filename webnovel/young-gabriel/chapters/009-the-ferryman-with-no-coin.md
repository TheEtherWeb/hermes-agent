# Chapter 9 — The Ferryman With No Coin

The river was wide.

It was wider than any water the boy had seen in his five-and-a-quarter years. The widow had told him once about the great middle sea, in the small evening way a mother tells a child about a thing she has never herself looked upon, and the boy had pictured the sea as a piece of water about the width of the millpond two ridges south of the village. He had been wrong. The river — and this was not even the sea — was, in front of him, a slow brown body of water perhaps two bowshots across, with the small lazy turning of a current that was not in a hurry and the small steady plip of fish coming up to take the morning flies.

The ferry was at the near bank.

The ferry was a flat raft of bound logs, sized for two oxen or one wagon or perhaps eight people standing close. There was a long pole at one corner. There were no oxen. There were no people. There was, on the small stool at the back of the raft, a man.

The man was the second-thinnest man the boy had ever seen.

The first-thinnest had been the old man who had come up out of the lower country two winters ago to sell salt. That old man had been thin from disease. This man, on the raft, was thin in a different way — thin from not having eaten well in a long time, but with the small thick wrists and shoulders of someone whose work was still the work of a strong man. He was, the boy guessed, perhaps thirty. He had a beard that had not been cut in many months. He had eyes the color of the river he was supposed to be ferrying across.

He was not, when the boy and Eshe walked up to the bank, ferrying.

He was sitting.

He did not look up.

The boy walked, with Eshe behind him, to the edge of the bank.

The boy said —

— *"Sir."*

The man said, without looking up —

— *"I cannot take you across."*

The boy said —

— *"Why."*

The man said —

— *"You have no coin."*

The boy said —

— *"You have not asked me whether I have coin."*

The man looked up.

He looked at the boy for a long time. He looked at Eshe. He looked, again, at the boy. The boy was, by then, five-and-a-quarter, and he was small for his age, and he was wearing a small wool tunic the color of dust, and he had on his hip a small wrap that had once been figs and cheese and was, now, mostly empty.

The man said —

— *"You have no coin."*

The boy said —

— *"You are right. But you did not need to look at me to know it. Why."*

The man was quiet for a while.

He said —

— *"No one has coin, anymore."*

The boy stepped onto the raft.

He stepped onto it the way a person steps onto any thing — without permission, but also without rudeness, the way the village's children had stepped onto Iossa's threshold to ask for the small bread-figures Iossa made every solstice. He sat down on the front of the raft, with his small bare feet hanging just above the water. Eshe, on the bank, did not follow. She watched.

The man said —

— *"Boy."*

The boy said —

— *"How long since anyone has paid you."*

The man said —

— *"Three years."*

The boy said —

— *"Why do you sit at the raft, then."*

The man said —

— *"It is the only thing I know how to do."*

The boy nodded.

He turned, on the raft, to look at the man.

He said —

— *"What is your name."*

The man said —

— *"My name is Lubo."*

The boy said —

— *"That is the name your mother gave you."*

The man said —

— *"It is."*

The boy said —

— *"It is not the name of the man who sits at this raft."*

The man was very still.

The boy looked at the long pole in the corner of the raft. He looked at the small worn place on the wood of the raft where Lubo's foot, over many years, had pressed down to push off from the bank. He looked at the small notch on the side of the raft where a coin-pouch had hung, once, but did not, anymore. He looked at the river.

He listened.

The river spoke to him in the slow long voice rivers have — slower than fire, faster than stone. The river said —

*he has ferried me without coin for three years. he has fed himself on the small fish at the bank and the bread of the women who pity him. he is the only man who has, in a hundred years, ferried me without taking. i have been waiting to give him a name.*

The boy said, in the small inward voice that was not quite speech —

*tell me.*

The river said —

*you tell him. i have only the shape. you have the mouth.*

The boy turned to Lubo.

He said —

— *"Onerai."*

The man — *Lubo,* by his mother, *Onerai* by the river — did not, for a moment, move.

He said —

— *"What."*

The boy said —

— *"That is the name of the man who sits at this raft. The man who ferries without coin. You have been him for three years. The river has known. It has been waiting to give you the name. I have come to give it to you."*

The man stood up.

He stood up slowly.

He walked to the front of the raft. He knelt down beside the boy. He put his face in his hands.

He was not weeping. He was, the boy saw, simply *being recognized*.

After a long while, he said —

— *"Onerai."*

The river — and the boy heard this, though Onerai did not — said —

*yes.*

The man said —

— *"What does it mean."*

The boy said —

— *"It means *the one who ferries without taking*. It is the name your work has been making for you, for three years. The work has been making it without you. Now you have it."*

The man said —

— *"I cannot eat a name."*

The boy said —

— *"No."*

The man said —

— *"I will starve, with a name."*

The boy said —

— *"You will not starve. Stand up. Take your pole. Ferry me across. Eshe with me. After today, when you ferry, the river will give to you what it has been wanting to give. The river is very tired of you eating only the bank-fish. The river has wider fish. The river will let you see them, in the shallows of the morning, in the way it has not let you see them before."*

The man stood up.

He took his pole.

He looked at the boy.

He said —

— *"Boy."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

The man said —

— *"Why."*

The boy said —

— *"Because the river asked. Because you have done a thing the world does not, anymore, do. Because a man who works for three years without coin is not a man with no name. He is a man whose name has been gathering, and someone had to come and put it in his mouth. I came. Eshe, come."*

Eshe stepped, in her unhurried way, onto the raft.

The raft sank a little under her weight, and then righted.

Onerai pushed off from the bank.

The pole went into the river, and the river — *and the boy heard this, but Onerai did not* — said, very softly —

*thank you.*

The raft moved out into the slow brown current.

It crossed at the slow speed of a man pushing a pole. The boy sat at the front and watched the far bank come closer. Eshe sat beside him with her face against his shoulder. The fish — and the boy noticed this — came up to the surface around the raft in a way they did not, in the boy's experience, come up to the surface around boats. They came up close. They surfaced beside Onerai's pole.

They were larger than the fish at the bank.

They were the kind of river-fish, the boy understood, the river had been keeping. They were the river's wages.

Onerai, by the middle of the crossing, was looking down at them with the small still face of a man seeing the small kind thing that has, for three years, been hidden from him.

He said, very quietly —

— *"How long have they been there."*

The boy said —

— *"They have always been there. The river was waiting until you were named."*

Onerai said —

— *"Why."*

The boy said —

— *"Because a man who does the work without taking is owed. The river does not owe a man with no name. It does not know who to pay. Now it knows."*

They reached the far bank.

The boy stepped off the raft. Eshe stepped off after him.

Onerai stood on his raft with his pole in his hands.

He said, to the boy —

— *"Will you tell me your name."*

The boy said —

— *"I do not have one yet."*

Onerai laughed. It was a small surprised laugh of a man who had not laughed in three years.

He said —

— *"A boy who names. A boy with no name."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes."*

Onerai said —

— *"When you have one, will you come back and tell me."*

The boy said —

— *"Yes. I promise."*

The boy meant it.

The river, in its slow speech, recorded the promise.

The boy would, in eighteen more years, come back. He would be a grown man, by then. He would be carrying his own self for the first time in his life. He would walk down to the bank of the river, and he would stand at the place Onerai's raft had once been moored, and he would find, instead of Onerai, a small stone monument with a name carved into it in Iossa's angular script.

The name on the stone would be *ONERAI*.

The villages, in eighteen years, would have learned how to write.

But that was eighteen years away, and the boy on the far bank was five-and-a-quarter, and Eshe was warm at his hip, and the road east continued.

He turned.

He waved.

Onerai, on his raft, waved back.

The boy walked east.

The salt in his hip-bag was a little less than it had been the morning of the wagon, because he had, before he stepped onto the raft, taken a small pinch and dropped it into the river. He had done it without thinking. He had done it the way one does an instinctive small courtesy. The river had taken the pinch with the small grateful warmth of a thing being thanked.

He would, in the years of his walking, do this at every river.

He had, on this morning, invented an offering.

He did not know it.

He had only, in his small ordinary five-and-a-quarter, given thanks.

The embers, in his chest, said —

*good, child. good. begin again.*

He began again.

He walked.
