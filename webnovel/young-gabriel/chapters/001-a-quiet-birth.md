# Chapter 1 — A Quiet Birth

She bore him by a fig tree.

The fig tree was not yet a fig tree on the morning she lay down beneath it. The fig tree was a tree, only, with a tree's name, which was *Tree*. It had not yet been told what kind. It had been waiting, in the patient way trees wait, for someone to come and finish it.

She did not know, when she lay down, that she was going to finish it.

She did not know much. She knew only that the pain had arrived earlier than the women had said it would, and that the women were two ridges away, and that the sun above her was, suddenly, not moving.

She looked up.

The sun, in the high white middle of a morning that had been midmorning a moment ago, was midmorning still. It had not slid the breadth of a thumb. It had not slid the breadth of a fingernail. It hung where it hung, as if it had forgotten the next thing.

She held her belly.

The contraction passed. Another came. She did not, anymore, count them. She had counted the first three because the women had told her to, and the women had said *count until they are four breaths apart and then send for me*, but the women were two ridges away and there was no one to send and the contractions had stopped having breaths between them at all.

She said, to no one — for there was no one to say it to, and she had been raised in a place that did not yet believe in saying things to no one —

— *"It is happening."*

The wind stopped to listen.

The wind had been moving across the ridge for days, carrying the smell of dry grass and the small fine dust the sheep raised at their grazing. The wind, at the sound of her sentence, set its load down. The dust, midair, hovered. The grass did not bend. The smell of the grass remained where it had been, perfectly, as if the wind had said *I will be back; do not move*.

She did not notice. She was breathing. Her breathing was the only sound she could hear, and that was right, because there was no other sound to hear. The sheep two ridges south had stopped. The birds in the lower olive grove had stopped. The cicada that had been arguing with itself in the brush above her head had stopped midsentence and would not finish that sentence until she was walking home.

The sea two days east stilled.

She did not know the sea was two days east, for she had never seen the sea, and she did not know what *stilling* was. But the sea stilled. Its surface, which had been a thing of waves for as long as there had been a sea, became, briefly, the color of a poured mirror. The fish, beneath, hung in place. A boy on a beach she would never visit stopped throwing pebbles, because the pebbles, when he threw them, did not break the water. They rested on top. He picked one up. He set it down. He did this for the length of the hour, and when his mother called him in for the evening meal he did not know how to say what had happened, because what had happened did not have, yet, a word.

The light, in the place she lay, gathered.

The light had, until that morning, been a thing of many sources — sun above, sky around, the small reflections off every white stone. The light, on the morning of her labor, forgot the other sources. The light came, instead, to her. The light became one thing in a place where it had been many. The light leaned in close. The light, in the moment of his crowning, cupped her face the way a woman cups the face of another woman whose child is being born.

She did not cry out. There was no sound. There was no breath.

The wind had set its breath down two ridges east, and she could not, in that moment, have summoned a breath of her own. She had not been given one. She did not need one.

She pushed.

He came.

He came without a cry. He came in silence so complete that the silence had a weight, and the silence, in its weight, was the first thing she handed him.

He took it.

He held it.

He did not, the way every other child she had ever heard come into the world had done, break it.

She lay back against the bark of the tree that was not yet a fig tree. The bark was warm. The bark had been cool an hour ago. The bark, now, was the warmth of something that had been waiting a long time for something else, and had finally been given the thing it was waiting for, and was answering the thing with all the heat it had saved.

She looked at his face.

His eyes were open.

His eyes were the color the light had been before the light had come to nurse her.

He looked at her.

He did not blink.

She said —

— *"Oh."*

The wind, two ridges east, picked up its breath again. The sea two days east remembered itself and resumed its long argument with its own shore. The sun above moved, very gently, the breadth of one fingernail. The cicada finished its sentence. The grass bent.

The fig tree, above her, finished.

She did not see it finish. She would not, in fact, ever see it finish. But the fig tree's leaves, which had been small and undecided, decided, in the second she set the child down on her chest. The leaves became their own shape. The bark became its own bark. The first small green knots of fruit appeared on the lower branches, where there had been, an hour before, only the question of fruit.

The tree had been, in its patient way, waiting for a witness.

The tree, on the morning of his birth, had been witnessed.

It was, from that morning forward, a fig tree.

She did not know to call it that. She would not know to call it that until much later, when she walked back down the ridge with the child in a wrap of her own undershirt, and a woman in the village she had been walking toward looked up from a bowl of soaked grain and said —

— *"You bore."*

She said —

— *"I bore."*

The woman said —

— *"Where."*

She said —

— *"Beneath a tree."*

The woman said —

— *"What tree."*

She did not know.

She did not know, and the woman in the village was the kind of woman who asked again when she had not been answered, and the woman opened her mouth to ask again, and then —

The child, on her chest, said the word.

The child, who was a half-hour old, said, in a voice no half-hour-old has any business owning, very softly, with the unhurried tone of someone naming a friend —

— *"Fig."*

The woman dropped her bowl.

The grain spilled on the dirt floor.

The grain, on the dirt, did not bounce.

It lay where it landed, perfectly still, as if it had been instructed.

She picked her child up. She held him to her shoulder. She did not speak. He did not speak again, that day. He would not speak again, in fact, for almost a year.

But the village, by sundown, knew.

The village did not yet know what it knew.

The village knew, only, that a thing had been named, and that the thing had agreed.
